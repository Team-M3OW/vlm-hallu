\begin{abstract}
Vision--language models on high-resolution imagery must decide where to spend a fixed visual-token budget;
evidence not given resolution is destroyed before the language model runs. The prevailing remedy is
attention-guided cropping: read the model's own attention over image positions, crop where it peaks, re-encode.
Every such method must choose a depth at which to read that map, and published methods hand-pick a single layer
or average a fixed block. We show this choice dominates every other design decision, and that the depth these
methods read is not the depth at which the image reaches the answer. Masking text-to-image attention layer by
layer localises that transport to the first two thirds of the stack: blocking every layer from layer~16 of~28
onward leaves the answer almost unchanged (KL $0.014$/$0.029$), and the boundary falls at the same fraction of
depth, $0.57$, on a $36$-layer checkpoint as on the two $28$-layer ones --- entirely before the band these
methods read.
Two policies follow from that measurement rather than from tuning. \textbf{Depth-Weighted Attention} (DWA)
replaces a hand-picked depth with one closed-form ridge solve over the whole depth profile. \textbf{Adaptive
Visual Resolution} (AVR) discards $90\%$ of visual tokens at the boundary, where they are causally inert, and
spends the freed compute on encoding resolution.
Across four architectures and four public benchmarks at full split ($4394$ items per model), DWA is worth
$+11.0$ to $+19.1$ points over spending the same tokens uniformly on the single-object strata those benchmarks
define for themselves, and beats the published read-out by up to $+14.8$ with no cell negative in twelve. Where
the incumbent fails it fails completely: on Gemma-3 its attention is indistinguishable from random
($1.1\times$ chance coverage) while re-weighting the \emph{same} attention is worth $+16.2$, and on CV-Bench's
counting split it under-counts by $1.29$ objects where re-weighting recovers $+27.9$. One property of the
question --- whether answering needs the whole scene --- predicts the sign of the effect everywhere we measure
and selects between the policies: cropping discards image \emph{area} and cannot serve scene-level questions,
while AVR discards only inert \emph{tokens}, keeps coverage, and is the better policy there in $6$ of $11$
conditions with no significant loss anywhere. The two are complementary by construction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A vision--language model asked ``what material is the glove?'' about a $2250\times1500$ photograph must first encode
the image into visual tokens. At any finite budget the glove may occupy a fraction of a token, and the evidence is
gone before the language model runs. The model still \emph{localises} it --- its attention concentrates on the right
region --- but it cannot \emph{perceive} it.

The standard remedy is attention-guided cropping: run the model once, read its attention over image positions, crop
where the attention peaks, and re-encode that crop at higher density~\citep{zhang2025vicrop,vicrop,zoomeye}. Every method in this
family faces one decision that is rarely examined: \textbf{at which layer should the attention map be read?}
Published methods pick a single mid-network layer or average a fixed block of late layers.

We show three things.

\textbf{(i) The depth choice dominates.} We crop at every one of the $28$ layers' arg-max in turn and answer, on both
checkpoints (Figure~\ref{fig:layeracc}). End-task accuracy ranges from $34.6\%$ to $70.2\%$ (Qwen3) and $36.1\%$ to
$62.8\%$ (Qwen2) --- spreads of $35.6$ and $26.7$ points driven by nothing but the depth at which the map is read.
Every layer inside the transport window scores below not cropping at all on both, and the published fixed-layer
recipe lands $32$ and $25$ points below the best layer. The same pipeline with a better depth read-out scores
$77.2\%$ and $71.3\%$ on the two checkpoints.

\textbf{(ii) Everyone reads at the wrong depth, and we can say why.} Masking text tokens' attention to the image,
layer by layer, shows that image-to-text transport is \emph{complete} by layer~16 of~28: blocking every layer from 16
onward leaves the output nearly unchanged (KL $0.014$/$0.029$; $12\%$/$5\%$ flips) and from layer~20 onward flips
essentially none. Yet every method in this family --- including ours --- reads its map at layers 17--22. The
attention these methods consume is a residue of where the model looked, not the channel carrying the evidence.

\textbf{(iii) Two allocation policies follow from that measurement}, with complementary strengths, and at native
resolution DWA matches a model spending $5.5$--$7.2\times$ the compute. \textbf{Depth-Weighted Attention} (DWA,
\S\ref{sec:ridge}) replaces a hand-picked depth with a single closed-form ridge solve over all of them. We test
what makes it work rather than asserting it: the conventional read-out fails because of a component of the map
that does not depend on the image, and the fit's job is to learn which handful of depths to read instead
(\S\ref{app:theory}).
\textbf{Adaptive Visual Resolution} (AVR, \S\ref{sec:tsr}) drops $90\%$ of visual tokens at the boundary, where
they no longer carry the image, and spends the saved compute on encoding resolution instead.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Attention-guided cropping.} \citet{zhang2025vicrop} establish the premise this family rests on --- a model's
attention concentrates on the right region even when its answer is wrong --- and turn it into a training-free crop,
evaluated over seven VQA datasets; \citet{liu2026vea} probe the same disconnect. ViCrop~\citep{vicrop,zhang2025vicrop}
crops at the peak of a fixed mid-layer attention map;
ZoomEye~\citep{zoomeye} searches a tree of crops with repeated model queries. VisLens~\citep{vislens} reaches the
same place without attention at all, decoding visual tokens through a tuned logit lens, matching them against the
query, and feeding the crop back alongside the original image in one pass. Every attention-based member fixes the
read-out depth by hand, and none of them budget-match against simply spending the same tokens on resolution ---
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows that at equal compute the family is worth $-1.6$ to $+3.1$ points.

\textbf{Layer-adaptive read-outs.} LASER~\citep{laser} selects the attention layer per sample by contrasting the map
with and without the query (VAQ), and reports that no single ``magic layer'' suffices --- a conclusion we reach
independently and extend causally. LASER does crop --- its title refers to static \emph{layers}, not to
cropping --- but it crops at \emph{half the image} with a $224{\times}224$ floor, and adds a VAT contrastive-decoding
stage. That window is four times the area of the one used throughout this literature, and by
\S\ref{sec:condition} window size is itself a determinant of whether cropping helps; we read it as prior art for
mitigating the coverage limit. Our tables implement LASER's localisation at our own window and without VAT, so
they isolate placement rather than evaluating the published system. ILVAD~\citep{ilvad} accumulates newly-activated tokens across adjacent layers;
TRACE~\citep{trace} identifies a three-stage depth pattern and reshapes it with trained modules; ViRGo~\citep{virgo}
reports our cross-instance finding (``patch-based zooming \ldots\ can split large objects and destroy global spatial
context'') and resolves it with a three-way router keyed on object scale. We treat these as concurrent work reaching compatible conclusions. Our contribution relative to them is
(i) the causal localisation of the transport window rather than a correlational choice of layer, (ii) a
\emph{supervised} read-out fitted over all depths rather than a rule chosen by hand, and (iii) budget-matched,
stratified evaluation including against native resolution.

\textbf{Depth selection in language models.} DoLa~\citep{dola} contrasts a mature and a premature layer; SLED~\citep{sled}
weights all early layers by gradient alignment; confident-layer decoding stops at an entropy valley near the output.
We port each to this setting and all fail (\S\ref{sec:neg}) for one measurable reason: in these vision--language
models final-layer entropy rises on $54$--$88\%$ of items --- far above the $16\%$ reported for language models ---
yet per-layer accuracy still climbs monotonically to the last layer, so on the \emph{decoding} axis there is no
better layer to select. The \emph{read-out} axis is different and we are explicit about it: a well-chosen fixed
layer does work (L17 scores $70.2\%$ against a $63.7\%$ no-crop bar), but it cannot be identified without labels,
the published choice is $31.9$ points worse than it, and LASER's per-sample layer selection reaches only $64.7\%$.
Re-weighting matches the oracle fixed layer without needing to know it.

\textbf{Adaptive acquisition.} AdaptVision~\citep{adaptvision} makes the allocation decision itself learned: the
model first sees a quarter-resolution image and autonomously emits a bounding-box tool call to crop from the
high-resolution original when it judges the coarse view insufficient, trained by RL with a balance reward that
penalises unnecessary acquisition. This is the trained counterpart of a gate we measure but cannot reach: a
per-item oracle choosing between the bar and AVR is worth $+3.0$ to $+13.1$ points on our grid (CI clear in
$11$ of $11$ cells), yet our label-free attempts to predict it reach AUROC $0.538$. We differ in kind rather than
degree. AdaptVision acquires \emph{more} tokens on demand and its refinement action is a crop, so it inherits the
coverage limit of \S\ref{sec:condition}; AVR encodes high and \emph{discards} at a boundary that is derived from a
causal measurement rather than trained, and keeps full coverage by construction. We regard learned acquisition as
the right way to close the gate, and a training-free method as the right way to establish where the gate should
point.

\textbf{Visual-token pruning.} FastV~\citep{chen2024fastv} prunes at layer~2; PyramidDrop~\citep{pyramiddrop} drops tokens in stages with a
fixed ratio; HiRED~\citep{hired} allocates a budget before the language model. These are evaluated as retention-at-reduced-cost. We
derive the pruning depth from the transport measurement rather than tuning it, and show that at that depth the
\emph{choice} of surviving tokens is irrelevant --- random keep matches attention keep --- while one layer earlier it
is worth $+8.4$ and $+8.9$ points.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Benchmarks.} \vstar~\citep{vstar} ($n=191$; 115 single-instance, 76 cross-instance) and HR-Bench 4K/8K
\citep{hrbench} ($n=800$ each; 400 per stratum). Both are public; we build no datasets. Questions are four-way
multiple choice, scored by the arg-max over option-letter log-probabilities, so results are deterministic.

\textbf{The equal-compute bar.} A two-pass method that localises at $300$ tokens and answers on a crop at $300$ tokens
spends $600$ visual tokens. We therefore compare against \texttt{uniform@600}: the same budget spent on one
higher-resolution pass. Published work in this area does not budget-match; \S\ref{sec:results} shows what that hides.
Where pruning changes cost mid-network we measure \textbf{token-layers} (tokens $\times$ layers traversed); the bar is
$600\times28=16{,}800$.

\textbf{Stratification.} Every result is reported separately for \emph{single-instance} questions (one object's
property) and \emph{cross-instance} questions (a relation between two). \S\ref{sec:scope} shows these behave
oppositely and that pooling them conceals it.

\textbf{Statistics.} Paired item bootstrap, 8000 resamples. Learned components use GroupKFold(5) by item $\times$ 3
seeds. A claim must hold on at least two checkpoints or it is reported as rejected.

\section{The depth at which the map is read dominates}\label{sec:depth}

Before proposing anything we establish the size of the effect that motivates it. We hold the pipeline
fixed --- same model, same question, same ${\sim}600$ visual-token budget, same fixed $W{=}0.25$ window ---
and vary \emph{only} the layer whose attention arg-max supplies the crop, sweeping all $28$ layers on both
checkpoints and answering at each.

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig_layer_accuracy2.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{End-task accuracy of cropping at each layer's arg-max, both checkpoints.} Every arm answers at
${\sim}600$ visual tokens, so only the read-out depth varies. The spread from depth alone is $35.6$ points on Qwen3
and $26.7$ on Qwen2, and inside the transport window no layer beats simply not cropping on either. The curve steps
up at the boundary and peaks at L17 (Qwen3) / L21 (Qwen2). DWA reads all $28$ layers and sits above every single
layer on both: against the best one --- chosen \emph{on the test set}, so an optimistic baseline --- it is $+2.6$
n.s.\ on Qwen3 and $+7.3$ [$+1.0,+13.6$] on Qwen2, i.e.\ it matches or beats an oracle choice of layer without being
told which layer that is. The published fixed choice (L14) is $24$--$32$ points below that oracle.}
\label{fig:layeracc}
\end{figure}

Three facts follow, and each is measured rather than argued. First, the spread attributable to depth alone is
$35.6$ points on Qwen3-VL-2B and $26.7$ on Qwen2-VL-7B --- larger than the gap between any two methods compared in
this literature. Second, \emph{every} layer inside the transport window scores below not cropping at all, on both
checkpoints: read the map too early and attention-guided cropping is actively harmful. Third, the fixed layer used
by published practice is $24$--$32$ points below the best layer, and in the pooled comparison of
Table~\ref{tab:main} it scores $38.2\%$ against a $63.9\%$ no-crop bar --- cropping at a hand-picked depth is worse
than not cropping.

This is the decision the family does not examine, and it dominates every other design choice in it. The rest of the
paper asks why the good depths are where they are (\S\ref{sec:transport}), and what follows from knowing
(\S\ref{sec:policies}).

\section{Where does the image actually reach the answer?}\label{sec:transport}

We install a hook that masks a chosen set of query positions from attending to the image-token columns, at chosen
layers only, and measure the KL divergence at the output.

\bottomrule

Blocking the answer-emission token from seeing any image token, at every layer, changes almost nothing on either
checkpoint (Table~\ref{tab:transport}). Visual evidence reaches the answer through the \emph{text} tokens, which consolidated it
upstream. Masking one layer at a time for all text rows localises that consolidation: it peaks at L11 (Qwen3) and
L14--15 (Qwen2), prefix masks $L_0..\ell$ saturate by $\ell=12$--$16$, and suffix masks $L_\ell..\text{end}$ fall to
$\approx0$ from $\ell=16$ ($0.014$ and $0.029$; essentially zero answer flips from $\ell=20$). The same fraction
transfers to a $36$-layer checkpoint under the same mask: its suffix is already nearly harmless at L20
($0.56\times$ depth; KL $0.052$, $5\%$ flips) and fully harmless from L24, and pruning at L21 --- the same fraction
--- is free ($-7.9$ points when the cut is instead placed at $0.14\times$ depth).

\paragraph{A second, independent causal test.} Masking shows the text-to-image attention \emph{channel} is inert
after the boundary. It does not show the image tokens' \emph{values} are. We therefore also replace the image
tokens' hidden states at layer $\ell$ with those computed from a \emph{different image} and measure the output
(Appendix~\ref{app:mech}). The effect falls by $17\times$ and $96\times$ between L12 and L16 and reaches
KL~$\approx 5\times10^{-4}$ with $0$--$2\%$ answer flips by L20, while two pre-registered controls stay large:
patching image tokens at L4, and patching \emph{text} tokens at L20. At L4 the image matters $151\times$ and
$131\times$ more than the text; at L20 the text matters $2492\times$ and $965\times$ more than the image. Two
different interventions, two checkpoints, one boundary.

\paragraph{The consequence.} The read-out band used by every method in this family (L17--20 on Qwen3, L19--22 on
Qwen2) lies \emph{entirely after} the transport window. Those layers' image attention is causally inert
(KL~$\approx0.001$). This predicts, and we confirm, three things: depth \emph{re-weighting} helps while depth
\emph{selection} never does (\S\ref{sec:ridge}); tokens can be dropped at the boundary for free (\S\ref{sec:tsr}); and
placement rules that all read the same inert depth separate only modestly.

\paragraph{Selection stops mattering exactly at the boundary.} Before it, the choice of which tokens to keep is worth
$+8.4$ and $+8.9$ points over random. At and after it, ranking is worth nothing: random keep equals attention keep on
both models at every keep ratio. The boundary is not merely where pruning becomes cheap --- it is where selection
ceases to matter.

\section{Two policies that follow from the measurement}\label{sec:policies}

Both policies below are derived from the boundary located in \S\ref{sec:transport} rather than tuned: one
replaces the hand-picked read-out depth, the other exploits the fact that visual tokens are causally inert past
the boundary. Neither introduces a hyperparameter chosen on test data.

\subsection{Depth-Weighted Attention (DWA)}\label{sec:ridge}

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig_pipe_ridge.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Policy I: Depth-Weighted Attention (DWA).} One localisation pass, every layer's attention kept, a single
closed-form solve that re-weights depths, and a crop. All $28$ layers enter the score. The fitted weights are
signed, but sign is not the mechanism: a non-negative refit retains the whole advantage while zeroing $44$ of $56$
per-layer coefficients (\S\ref{app:theory}).}
\label{fig:pipe_ridge}
\end{figure}


Localise at $300$ tokens with the full prompt (the read-out must be taken at the answer-emission position; without the
answer instruction the map loses $36$--$42$ points). Read the final prompt token's attention over image cells at all
$28$ layers and normalise each layer's map over the cells, $A_\ell(c)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:feat}). Score each cell by
a single closed-form ridge solve over its depth profile,
\[
s(c)=w_0+\textstyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1} w_\ell\log A_\ell(c),
\]
fit on as few as $50$ boxed examples. The deployed read-out additionally carries rank, neighbourhood and geometry
features --- $\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{2L+7}$, specified in Appendix~\ref{app:feat} --- and a leave-one-group-in
ablation shows the signed log-attention block alone carries the advantage. Crop $W{=}0.25$ at the arg-max, re-encode
at $300$ tokens, answer.

The fitted $w_\ell$ \emph{is} the depth filter (Figure~\ref{fig:filter}). It takes both signs: it assigns
\emph{negative} weight to 7 of the 11 layers the conventional block-mean read-out adds at $+1$, and its largest
positive weight falls in the band where attention first becomes question-conditioned. There is no boosting, no
learning rate, no early stopping, no seed averaging --- given the data the solution is unique.

\paragraph{Why re-weighting and not selection.} On the items the conventional read-out gets wrong, the learned head
ranks the target cell above the cell the arg-max chose on $57\%$ and $71\%$ of items (gradient-boosted; the deployed
ridge ties it as a ranker, Appendix~\ref{app:paired}), against $13\%$ and $12\%$ for the block mean. None of the
simple summaries of the depth profile reproduces this --- not magnitude, neighbourhood, isolation, or a windowed sum.
The mechanism is a \emph{supervised depth weighting}, not subtraction: the block mean's failure is caused by the
item-independent component of the maps, and a non-negative fit --- which zeroes $44$ of $56$ per-layer coefficients
--- retains the full advantage on both checkpoints and matches DWA at the end task (Appendix~\ref{app:theory}).

\paragraph{Ablations.} A gradient-boosted tree over the same features is statistically indistinguishable from the
ridge solve on all four architectures, so the closed form costs nothing. Capacity is not the limit at this sample size: a
spatial CNN is \emph{significantly worse} on the 7B ($-8.9$ and $-9.4$ at two window sizes), and nine random seeds,
flip augmentation and ensembling all land inside the noise floor. Roughly $50$ boxed examples recover $70\%$ of the
full-data gain. A training-free variant --- the maximum over the four question-conditioned layers --- reaches $68.9$
and $60.7$: above every published rule on Qwen3, and above the block-mean and fixed-layer rules on Qwen2 (LASER
reaches $61.3$ there), but $3.7$ and $9.5$ points below the fitted filter.

\subsection{Adaptive Visual Resolution (AVR)}\label{sec:tsr}

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig_pipe_tsr.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Policy II, adaptive visual resolution.} Spend the compute the inert late-layer visual tokens
would have used on resolution inside the window where the image is actually read. The prune depth is read off the
causal measurement rather than tuned, and nothing spatial is discarded before the boundary.}
\label{fig:pipe_tsr}
\end{figure}


Because visual tokens are inert past the boundary, discard them there and spend the saving on pixels: encode at $900$
tokens, run full width through L0--L16, keep the top $10\%$ at L16, run L17--27 narrow. Token-layers
$900{\times}17+90{\times}11=16{,}290$ --- $96\%$ of the bar, for $1.5\times$ the resolution. No crop.

Pruning is free to within $\pm0.5$ points in $7$ of the $10$ measured cells (two more are ahead by $1.3$ and $2.6$,
read as noise), at every keep ratio ($10/25/50\%$), with any ranking including random; one cell shows a real cost
(HR-8K cross-instance, $-1.8$). Output probabilities change on $99\%$ of items while the arg-max changes on $2\%$ ---
the tokens are inert, not absent.

\paragraph{An identity.} Across $4$ checkpoints $\times$ $3$ benchmarks, AVR's gain equals the checkpoint's
\emph{measurable resolution headroom} $\mathrm{acc}(900)-\mathrm{acc}(600)$ on that stratum: $r=0.966$, slope $1.01$,
mean absolute error $0.7$ points over 10 stratum-cells. The headroom needs two baseline runs and no method at all, so
AVR's benefit is \emph{predictable before deployment} --- and it explains the cells where AVR gains little: those are
the cells where the checkpoint gains little from resolution in the first place, including one where the baseline
itself degrades from $600$ to $900$ tokens.

\paragraph{The boundary is the right depth, not merely a cheap one.} One cut at the boundary dominates the schedules
we tried: a taper that prunes inside the transport window loses on both checkpoints ($-1.0$ Qwen3, $-5.8$ Qwen2), and
on the 36-layer checkpoint a voided run that cut at $0.14$ of depth instead of $0.57$ cost $7.9$ points pooled
(Appendix~\ref{app:faults}). The FastV default (layer~2) is the exception that proves the point: it costs $4.7$ on
Qwen3 but is the better schedule on Qwen2, whose resolution curve is flat beyond $600$ tokens --- the best fixed
schedule differs by checkpoint, so we claim only that the cut must not fall inside the window. Pruning \emph{after}
the boundary saves less for no additional accuracy.

\subsection{The failure of cropping is the argument for AVR}\label{sec:avrmotive}

The condition above is not a caveat to be lived with; it is a specification. Cropping fails on
scene-level questions for one reason --- it discards $93.75\%$ of the image \emph{area} --- and that
reason is structural, so no placement rule can repair it. What the regime needs is a policy that
buys resolution \emph{without} discarding coverage. AVR is exactly that policy: it keeps the whole
image and discards visual \emph{tokens} at the transport boundary, where \S\ref{sec:transport} shows
they no longer carry the image to the answer. The two policies are therefore complementary by
construction rather than by tuning, and the prediction is sharp: AVR should not exhibit the loss
that cropping exhibits, on precisely the questions that cause it.

Together the two policies cover the space. Ask for a local property of one region and DWA is worth
up to $+19.1$ points (Table~\ref{tab:working}); ask a question of the scene and AVR holds the line
where cropping loses double digits. Both follow from the same measurement --- one uses the boundary
to choose \emph{where} to read the map, the other uses it to choose \emph{what} may be discarded ---
and the question property that selects between them is observable before either is run.

This is an actionable rule rather than a caveat: apply attention-guided cropping when the question
asks about a local property of one region, and do not apply it otherwise. On the strata that satisfy
it, Table~\ref{tab:working} shows the method is worth up to $19$ points.

\subsection{A per-item policy with no question classifier}\label{sec:policy}

The rule above needs to know whether the question is local, and a deployed system must decide per
item, before either pass. Our standing constraint forbids a question classifier; the decision is
instead available from the same localisation pass, label-free. We compute three statistics that each
track whether a crop has something to find: $\mathrm{disp}$, the ring-masked top-1 mass share of the
block-mean map; $\mathrm{band\_disp}$, the same on the read-out-band mean map; and $\mathrm{VRH}$, the
block-mean mass inside the proposed crop's $W{=}0.25$ window. Each is thresholded at its median over
the incoming item set --- label-free and parameter-free --- and the policy crops (DWA at $300$) only
when \emph{all three} agree; otherwise it keeps the whole image and buys resolution (AVR where the
checkpoint has a ladder, the equal-compute bar otherwise).

given the same compute. InternVL3's $8.37\times$ ratio is itself an artefact worth naming: its ladder
jumps from $256$ tokens to $1792$, so a $600$-token target lands on $256$ while the crop costs
$1792$. We report the matched column throughout and recommend it be required.

window does exist, the relation is still not recovered from the magnified view (Appendix~\ref{app:neg}) --- placement
is not the binding constraint there. AVR shows the opposite
profile --- positive on both strata in $6/7$ cells with no significant loss anywhere --- but gives up $3$--$10$
points of single-instance gain.

The ceiling explains why cropping cannot be repaired here. With \emph{perfect} placement, cross-instance still fails
$25\%$ and $22\%$ of the time against $3.5\%$ and $6.1\%$ for single-instance. The residual is not predominantly
perceptual, and no crop, multi-crop, composition or pruning schedule reached it (\S\ref{sec:neg}). The general form
of the condition --- the whole scene is required --- is measured directly in \S\ref{sec:condition}.


\subsection{Practical guidance}\label{sec:guide}

The two policies have different cost and risk profiles, and the choice does not require a question classifier. The
DWA needs ${\sim}50$ boxed examples, two forward passes and $1.4$--$2.0\times$ wall-clock; it has the higher
ceiling ($+14.8$ on single-instance) and a $6$--$9$ point cross-instance failure mode. AVR needs no supervision, one
forward pass and ${\sim}1\times$ wall-clock; it is positive on both strata in 6 of 7 cells with no significant loss
anywhere, and its benefit is predictable from two baseline runs. Head to head the two are even pooled (mean
difference $+0.2$ points over seven cells). We recommend AVR as the default and DWA where boxed
supervision exists and single-instance perception dominates. We note that DWA's pooled margin over AVR
is concentrated on the two cells on which it was developed.

\subsection{VLMs and Fine-Grained Perception: Visual Resolution as an Encoding Bottleneck}\label{sec:cliff}

We study whether VLMs can resolve small visual details after correctly localising the relevant region, and whether allocating visual tokens to the relevant region is more effective than uniformly increasing resolution. We first sweep the target extent in merged visual tokens on single-region questions, using an oracle crop as a difficulty control (Appendix~\ref{app:cliff}). We find a sharp, architecture-invariant threshold at 0.15--0.25 merged tokens, corresponding to only 2--3\% of the image side: accuracy jumps from 19.0\% below to 78.6\% above the threshold, while oracle-crop accuracy remains flat (95.8\%/100.0\%). Since question difficulty is unchanged across the split, the cliff reflects a failure to encode the required visual evidence rather than a gradual loss of accuracy with decreasing resolution.

We next localise the source of this failure using a logit lens and representation patching (Appendices~\ref{app:mech} and~\ref{app:cliff}). Under the uniform encoding no layer's decoded answer exceeds 52.9\%, and on localised-but-wrong items the correct option is the argmax at some layer on only 72.2\% of items, against 68.1\% where the window missed entirely: localisation buys almost nothing in internal decodability. No layer shows the answer present and then lost --- the apparent peak at L20 (41.7\%) collapses to 0\% from L22 and matches what near-chance layers produce. Under the oracle crop the answer instead becomes decodable abruptly at L22, taking 90\% of the gain in a single transition and reaching 92.6\%: sufficient visual detail does not make the model reason better, it supplies information that a fixed depth converts into an answer. The step replicates one layer later on Qwen2-VL-7B (L22--23), and only where the crop covers the target (+23.8 points covered, exactly 0.0 missed). The oracle crop corrects 94.4\% of localised-but-wrong cases at a median of only 0.14 tokens on target; across models, 86.7\%/84.0\% of errors are therefore certified as encoding failures, indicating that the dominant bottleneck is extracting fine-grained visual evidence rather than reasoning over evidence that has already been encoded.

This raises a practical question: should we increase the overall visual-token budget, or allocate the same budget more selectively? We compare oracle-targeted allocation against the best uniform resolution each model can reach, across three architectures at native resolution and the HR-Bench boundary (Appendix~\ref{app:cliff}). Targeted allocation is substantially more efficient: a 300-token oracle crop reaches 97.4\%/93.2\%, while no uniform setting up to 7{,}776 tokens matches it ($\geq$26$\times$/25.9$\times$ more tokens). At native resolution, a 600-token crop likewise outperforms 3,290 uniform tokens by +11.6/+16.2 points. Crucially, the benefit depends on whether the evidence is actually sub-token: targeted allocation improves V*Bench by +37.2 points, where fine details are difficult to resolve, but hurts HR-Bench 4K by $-8.8$ points when the evidence is already resolvable.

\section{What does not work}\label{sec:neg}

Ten designs failed to make cropping serve cross-instance questions, and two more failed to combine the two
policies into a single pipeline. We report them because the diagnoses
constrain the design space more than the successes do. Each failed for a measured reason: per-item window
sizing; peak-spanning and mass-containment windows; joint (cell, window) re-ranking under a coverage objective (a
metric artefact --- coverage is monotone in window size); expected-accuracy window selection; a downscaled scene
composed with the crop, which dilutes it by $8.7$ and $5.2$ points \emph{even at oracle placement}; multi-crop at matched
budget, where the second crop genuinely converts ($+7.8$\,\ok) but halving each crop costs $-11.3$; and the same at
full resolution funded by pruning. Depth \emph{selection} rules ported from the LLM literature
--- DoLa, SLED, confident-layer decoding, attention-JSD, rank-stability --- all fail, for one reason: in these models
final-layer entropy rises on $54$--$88\%$ of items while per-layer accuracy still climbs monotonically to the last
layer. There is no better layer to select.

\textbf{Eight internal interventions, eight nulls.} A separate pattern is now complete: residual-stream steering,
attention amplification, contrastive decoding on \vstar\ and on CV-Bench, self-confidence gating, wrongness
prediction (AUROC $0.538$), unsupervised placement, and hidden-state probes. Each is indistinguishable from its
matched random control at the end task --- in the most recent case a DWA-masked logit contrast trails a
\emph{random}-masked one at every contrast strength ($-0.3$ to $-1.6$ points), so the economics of the contrast are
not what limits it. The consistency is itself the result, and it is the strongest evidence for the paper's central
methodological claim: \textbf{allocation must happen in pixel space}. Choosing what to \emph{encode} is what buys
accuracy; re-weighting, amplifying or decoding what was already encoded does not. One experiment said this first;
eight now do, on two benchmarks and four architectures.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:lim}

$n=191$ on \vstar\ gives per-stratum CI half-widths near $10$ points; no method is significantly positive on both
strata in any single cell, which is a power limitation as much as a method one. DWA needs ${\sim}50$
boxed examples and two forward passes; AVR needs neither.
DWA's pooled margin over AVR is concentrated on the two cells it was developed on.
Pruning at the boundary is free in 9 of 10 cells; the exception (HR-Bench~8K, cross-instance, $-1.8$\,\no) is real.

\textbf{The two claims have different scope, and we do not merge them.} That depth re-weighting beats the
published single-block read-out holds at matched cost on four of five architectures
(Table~\ref{tab:families}); on LLaVA-OneVision it is positive but not significant. That cropping is
worth its compute \emph{at all}, against a bar given the same total budget, holds on only three of
five, failing on the two families with unspent resolution headroom. A practitioner whose checkpoint
can still buy resolution should buy resolution first.

\textbf{The transfer arm is adopted on a threshold it clears exactly.} Its pre-registered rule --- $\lvert$DWA
$-$ block$\rvert$ excluding zero on ${\geq}2$ of $4$ Qwen cells --- passes $2$ of $4$, and the split is not random:
both Qwen2-VL-7B cells pass and both Qwen3-VL-2B cells fail. Stratified, the single-instance margin is
the consistent quantity ($+9.5$, $+5.0$, $+10.5$, $+10.0$; three of four CIs clear).

\textbf{Two of the five families required a measured token layout before any method could be run at
all}, and a wrong layout is empirically indistinguishable from an incompatible architecture: our
first attempt put Gemma-3 and LLaVA-NeXT at $1.1\times$ and $1.14\times$ chance and we wrongly concluded
both were incompatible. Attention-based allocation is more sensitive to visual-token layout than this
literature acknowledges, and we report the layout we measured for each family rather than deriving it
from tile arithmetic (Appendix~\ref{app:faults}).

\section{Conclusion}

Attention-guided cropping reads the model's attention at a depth where that attention no longer carries the image to
the answer. Measuring where it does --- a causal masking experiment, not a heuristic --- yields both a better
read-out (DWA) and a free pruning point (AVR), and the two together match a model running at $5.5\times$ the
visual-token budget.
The remaining gap is placement: perfect placement at $600$ tokens exceeds native dynamic resolution by $11.6$ points.

\section{Experimental protocol}\label{app:proto}

\textbf{Models.} Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct (core pair), Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct
(36 layers), LLaVA-NeXT-7B and LLaVA-OneVision-7B (attention analyses only). \texttt{bfloat16}; eager attention
wherever hooks or a prune bias are required, SDPA otherwise. We note a ${\sim}2$ point kernel-dependent difference on
the 7B between the two implementations and always compare arms computed under the same kernel.

\textbf{Budgets.} Images are resized so that the processor's own \texttt{image\_grid\_thw} yields the target token
count ($\pm 8\%$); realised counts are recorded per arm and a contrast is voided if budgets drift by more than $10\%$.
Where pruning changes cost mid-network we report token-layers.

\textbf{Read-out.} The attention map is the final prompt token's attention over image-token columns, averaged over
heads \emph{before} normalising (the order matters: normalising per head first up-weights sink-heavy heads and shifts
the block-mean read-out by $11$ points on the 7B). The prompt must end at the answer-emission point; a prompt ending
elsewhere costs $36$--$42$ points on the arg-max read-out.

\textbf{Statistics.} Paired item bootstrap with 8000 resamples. Learned components use GroupKFold(5) grouped by item
$\times$ 3 seeds; the headline DWA result is additionally reproduced under a disjoint fold-seed set (difference
within $\pm1$ point). Run-to-run floor on coverage is $1$--$2.5$ points.

\textbf{Pipeline discipline.} An exactly $+0.0$ contrast with a $[+0.0,+0.0]$ interval is treated as a pipeline fault,
never a result; this rule caught a monkey-patch that silently failed to reach one model family. Every pruning run
asserts at runtime that the loaded model's attention module is the one that was patched.

\section{The DWA read-out: features, estimator, and the feature-block ablation}\label{app:feat}

Let $a_i^{(\ell)}$ denote the head-mean attention at image cell $i$ and layer $\ell$, where
$i=1,\ldots,N$ and $\ell=0,\ldots,L-1$, read from the final prompt token over the image cells. We normalise
each map over the image cells,
\begin{equation}
    A_i^{(\ell)} = \frac{a_i^{(\ell)}}{\max\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_j^{(\ell)},\epsilon\right)},
    \qquad \epsilon=10^{-12},
\end{equation}
where $j$ indexes image cells in the sum. Each cell is represented by its normalised depth profile,
$\bigl(\log(A_i^{(\ell)}+\epsilon)\bigr)_{\ell=0}^{L-1}$, so with $L=28$ each cell has $28$ features. The read-out
is a single closed-form ridge regression on these features,
\begin{equation}
    w = (X^\top X + \alpha I)^{-1} X^\top y, \qquad \alpha=1,
\end{equation}
with an unpenalised intercept, fitted out-of-fold (GroupKFold(5) $\times$ 3 group permutations, grouped by item)
to the coverage label $y_i$, the fraction of the ground-truth box inside the $W{=}0.25$ window centred at cell
$i$. Placement is the ring-masked arg-max of the score $s(c) = w_0 + \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1} w_\ell \log A_\ell(c)$.

\textbf{The deployed vector.} The deployed read-out carries two further blocks,
$\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{2L+7}$: (i) $L$ per-layer rank features,
$R_i^{(\ell)}=\mathrm{rank}_\downarrow\!\bigl(a_i^{(\ell)}\bigr)/(N-1)$, the cell's descending rank in layer
$\ell$'s map; and (ii) seven neighbourhood/geometry features --- the log $3\times3$ neighbourhood mean of the
read-out-band map (the band where attention first becomes question-conditioned,
$L_{\mathrm{lo}}=\mathrm{round}(0.57L)$ through $L-1$), the cell's horizontal and vertical coordinates, its
distance from the image centre, its distance to the nearest border, and two indicators for the last row and
column. Ranks are per-layer and item-relative; the geometry block is item-independent.

removed, out-of-fold coverage of the $W{=}0.25$ window at the ring-masked arg-max:

\section{Intermediate metrics systematically over-report}\label{app:proxy}

Five times in this work an intermediate metric improved while end-task accuracy did not: a read-out that closed the
coverage gap but converted $3.1$ points worse; a ridge variant that tied on coverage and differed on the chosen cell
for two-thirds of items; an early-exit localiser free in coverage but $-3.7$ pooled at the end task; a
higher-resolution localiser that gained $+4.2$ coverage and $-3.7$ accuracy; and an objectness probe that retains the
evidence box far better than attention ($87\%$ vs.\ $72\%$) and converts to $-1.6$. We therefore report coverage only
as a diagnostic and gate every claim on end-task accuracy at matched compute.

\section{Propositions, and what validates them}\label{app:theory}

We state four theorems, three propositions and two lemmas. They are not deep results --- two are tail-dominance and
sufficiency statements, two are information bounds, and the lemmas are one-line linear algebra --- but each makes a
prediction that could have failed on data, and we report where they hold approximately rather than exactly. All measurements are out-of-fold on both core
checkpoints; $n=126$ (modal grid) for coverage, $n=191$ for end-task and distributional tests.

\textbf{Setup.} For item $i$, layer $\ell$, cell $c$, let $A_\ell^{(i)}(c)\ge0$ be the last-prompt-token attention
mass on cell $c$, row-normalised so $\sum_cA_\ell^{(i)}(c)=1$. Write $m_\ell=\mathbb{E}_iA_\ell^{(i)}$ for the
\emph{item-mean} (item-independent) map and $A_\ell^{(i)}=m_\ell+U_\ell^{(i)}$, so $\mathbb{E}_iU_\ell^{(i)}=0$.

\begin{theorem}[Tail dominance of the conventional read-out]\label{thm:tail}
Write each depth's per-cell score as $x_\ell(c)=m_\ell(c)+u_\ell(c)$ with $u_\ell$ zero-mean over items, and put
$\Delta_B=\max_c\sum_{\ell\in B}m_\ell(c)-\mathrm{med}_c\sum_{\ell\in B}m_\ell(c)$. The block read-out returns
$\arg\max_c\sum_{\ell\in B}x_\ell(c)$.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
\item[(i)] If $\Delta_B$ exceeds the range of $\sum_{\ell\in B}u_\ell$, the arg-max is
  $\arg\max_c\sum_B m_\ell$ for \emph{every} item: a fixed cell independent of the image.
\item[(ii)] Equal-weight positive averaging cannot escape (i) at \emph{any} band width: the $m_\ell$ leads are
  sign-aligned across depth, so $\Delta_B$ is superadditive in $B$.
\item[(iii)] Any per-cell rescaling that equalises $m$ across cells --- in particular dividing by an estimate of
  $m$ --- removes the dominance without using labels.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(i) If the deterministic part's lead exceeds the total variation the item-specific part can supply, it decides the
maximum. (ii) Each summand contributes its $m_\ell$ lead with the same sign, so $\Delta_B$ is superadditive; whether
$\Delta_B/\sigma_B$ rises or falls depends on the covariance of the $u_\ell$, and the claim is only that it does not
reach the $O(1)$ regime in which $\sum_B u_\ell$ could decide the arg-max. (iii) Immediate from (i) applied to the
rescaled scores.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Leakage decomposition]\label{lem:leak}
For every weight vector $w$, $\sum_\ell w_\ell A_\ell^{(i)}=L(w)+\sum_\ell w_\ell U_\ell^{(i)}$ with
$L(w)=\sum_\ell w_\ell m_\ell$ independent of $i$. The item-independent component of the score is exactly $L(w)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Substitute the decomposition and rearrange. $L(w)$ carries no $i$-dependence, so its ranking of cells is identical
for every item.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Non-negative filters cannot cancel a common prior]\label{lem:nn}
If the item-mean maps are rank-one across depth, $m_\ell=\alpha_\ell\mu$ with $\mu\neq0$ and $\alpha_\ell>0$, then
$L(w)=(\alpha^\top w)\mu$. Hence $L(w)=0$ iff $\alpha^\top w=0$; no non-zero $w\ge0$ achieves zero leakage.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Linearity, then $\alpha^\top w=\sum_\ell\alpha_\ell w_\ell>0$ whenever $w\ge0$, $w\neq0$.
\end{proof}

\emph{Every part is measured, and the measurements decide between accounts that (ii) alone cannot separate.}
On the items the block mean gets wrong, the cell it selects sits at the \textbf{100th percentile} of the
item-independent map --- on both checkpoints, on every failure. It is not mis-weighting evidence; it is returning
the raster sink. Consistent with (ii), $\Delta_B/\sigma_B$ measured over band widths $1$ to $11$ stays between $10.2$ and $16.6$
on one checkpoint and between $7.0$ and $15.1$ on the other --- always an order of magnitude above the regime
where the item-specific term could win, so no amount of averaging rescues the rule. The ratio \emph{rises} with
width on the first checkpoint and \emph{falls} on the second, so we claim only that averaging never escapes, not
that wider is monotonically worse. What is common to both is that the deployed eleven-wide band is worse than the
best narrower band ($0.418\to0.083$ and $0.390\to0.297$), and that its last-column rate is higher there
($17.5\to91.3\%$ and $45.2\to62.7\%$). Consistent with (iii), an \emph{unsupervised} correction --- divide each cell by its value on a typical
item, then take the arg-max --- lifts coverage from $0.083$ to $0.408$ and from $0.292$ to $0.504$, reaching
$71\%$ and $86\%$ of what the fitted read-out achieves, and drops the last-column rate to $2.4\%$ and $0.0\%$
against a $4.8\%$ chance rate, with no ring mask.

\emph{And it buys nothing at the end task, which bounds what this proposition explains.} Run as a placement rule
and answered, the same label-free correction scores $59.7\%$ and $61.3\%$ against the block mean's $62.3\%$ and
$59.2\%$: $-2.6$ [$-7.9,+2.6$] and $+2.1$ [$-3.1,+7.3$], neither clear of zero, and it does not beat not cropping
at all. DWA beats it by $+13.1$ [$+5.8,+20.4$] and $+8.9$ [$+2.6,+15.2$], clear on both checkpoints. So the
proposition explains why the conventional read-out \emph{places} badly, and a label-free fix repairs the
placement, but repairing placement is not what the fitted read-out is selling. Its end-task advantage survives
after the placement failure has been removed by other means, and we do not yet have a mechanism for that
remainder. This is also the largest coverage-to-accuracy gap we have measured --- $66$--$70\%$ of the coverage gap
closed, none of the accuracy gap --- and it is a caution that applies to our own use of coverage in this section,
not only to prior work.

\emph{What the fit adds, and why its sign does not matter.} Because the features are log-attention, a weight
vector summing to zero computes a \emph{ratio} across depths, which cancels any per-cell factor common to all
layers; the fitted weights are close to that, with $|\sum_\ell w_\ell|/\sum_\ell|w_\ell| = 0.126$ and $0.225$.
But that is not the only route. Sink dominance itself varies $3.2\times$ and $4.4\times$ across depth, and the
least-dominated depths are exactly the read-out band, so a strictly non-negative read-out can escape by
\emph{selecting} those depths rather than by cancelling anything. Both routes are available, which is why
constraining every weight to be non-negative costs nothing while zeroing $44$ and $43$ of $56$ coefficients. The
item-mean maps are approximately rank-one (leading eigenvalue share $0.86$ and $0.77$), and in map space the
non-negative constraint costs only $0.044$ and $0.002$ mean coverage: the sign of the fitted weights is an artefact
of ridge collinearity between adjacent layers, not the mechanism. We do
not claim the fitted weights track dominance depth-by-depth: that correlation is only $+0.171$ and $+0.131$. The
supported statement is that the failure is tail dominance, that it has an unsupervised fix which recovers most of
the gap, and that supervision buys the remaining $14$--$29\%$.

\emph{A refinement the theory predicted, which failed.} If what remains after dividing out $m$ were
heteroscedastic across cells, the right label-free statistic would be the studentised one, $(x(c)-m(c))/s(c)$. We
pre-registered that it would beat the plain ratio. It does not, on either checkpoint ($-0.015$ and $-0.028$), and
it closes $63\%$ and $61\%$ of the gap against the plain ratio's $66\%$ and $70\%$. The proposition therefore
explains the failure and supplies a label-free correction, but does not yet predict a better one: the residual
that supervision buys is not simple heteroscedasticity.

\emph{Two accounts we tested first and rejected.} That signed weights cancel a common nuisance: refuted, since
non-negative fits lose nothing and the orthogonality the argument needs measures $+0.431$ and $+0.388$ rather than
zero. That the fit is a matched filter over depth, weighting by per-layer signal-to-noise: refuted, one of two on
each of its predictions, and wrong in kind --- the block mean already retains $77\%$ of attainable signal-to-noise
on both models while its coverage is several times worse, and a mean-based quantity cannot explain an arg-max
failure.

\begin{theorem}[A single-depth read-out is lossy unless that depth is sufficient]\label{thm:suff}
Let $c^\star$ be the cell containing the evidence and $A=(A_1,\dots,A_L)$ the depth profile. For any rule
$\hat c=g(A_\ell)$ reading a single layer: (i) $c^\star\to A\to A_\ell\to\hat c$ is a Markov chain, so
$I(c^\star;\hat c)\le I(c^\star;A_\ell)\le I(c^\star;A)$; (ii) the right inequality is an equality iff
$c^\star\perp A\mid A_\ell$, i.e.\ $A_\ell$ is sufficient for $c^\star$ within the profile; (iii) by Fano,
$\Pr[\hat c\neq c^\star]\ge(H(c^\star)-I(c^\star;A_\ell)-1)/\log|\mathcal{C}|$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$A_\ell$ is a coordinate projection of $A$ and $\hat c$ is post-processing of $A_\ell$, so the chain holds and
the data-processing inequality applies along each link, giving (i). For (ii),
$I(c^\star;A)-I(c^\star;A_\ell)=I(c^\star;A\mid A_\ell)\ge0$ by the chain rule, with equality exactly under
conditional independence. (iii) is Fano applied to that chain.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:suff} is vacuous until sufficiency is decided, and sufficiency is an empirical property of a
given model, so we test its equality condition. If $A_\ell$ were sufficient its arg-max would be correct
wherever any layer's is, so the best single depth would match the family's envelope. Cropping at every one of
the $28$ layers' arg-max and answering gives:

\begin{theorem}[Decoding bound, and a certified gap for the conventional read-out]\label{thm:info}
For any read-out decision $\hat c$ of a per-cell score and target cell $T$:
(i) $I(\hat c;T)\le H(\hat c)\le\log|\mathcal C|$;
(ii) $I(\hat c;T)\ge H(T)-H_b(P_e)-P_e\log(|\mathcal C|-1)$ with $P_e=\Pr[\hat c\ne T]$;
(iii) if $\hat c$ is constant then $I(\hat c;T)=0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(i) $I(\hat c;T)=H(\hat c)-H(\hat c\mid T)\le H(\hat c)\le\log|\mathcal C|$. (ii) Fano's inequality.
(iii) $H(\hat c)=0$, so (i) gives $I=0$.
\end{proof}

The bounds are exact and directly measurable, and the same maps admit an oracle decision that \emph{is} the target,
so $I(\text{oracle};T)=H(T)=6.06$ bits on the modal-grid items. The conventional read-out does not reach that: the
literal block mean (no ring mask) has decision entropy $1.02$ and $3.46$ bits on the two checkpoints and lands in
the grid's last column on $84.8\%$ and $61.8\%$ of the full item set, so it can carry at most that much --- a
certified gap of $\ge5.0$ and $\ge2.6$ bits. The ring mask restores the baseline's decision entropy ($6.30$/$4.60$
bits) but not its accuracy; the fitted read-out attains the same entropy with higher coverage ($0.605$/$0.581$
against $0.459$/$0.402$). The failure is in the decoder, not the representation. Two consequences. The information
a decision can carry is raised only by making it \emph{varied} or \emph{accurate} --- the bounds never mention
cancellation --- so a non-negative depth fit reaches the same terms by selecting the depths where the
item-independent component is least dominant (\S\ref{sec:ridge}). And the depth \emph{ordering} changes both terms
while the sign of the weights does not.

\begin{theorem}[The information horizon]\label{thm:horizon}
Suppose the visual-token values after layer $p$ are output-invariant:
$I(V_{>p};Y\mid\text{state}_p)=0$ (value-inertness). Then (i) pruning to any keep-set loses no answer
information; (ii) AVR's gain over the bar $E_0$ is the resolution information gain
$\mathrm{acc}(E)-\mathrm{acc}(E_0)$, bounded by $I(X;Y)-I(Z_{E_0};Y)$; and (iii) the information-bearing depth is
$p+1$ layers, so the affordable resolution multiplier is $N/(p+1)=1.647\times$ over uniform-depth encoding.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(i) The premise makes the answer conditionally independent of those values given the state at $p$, and the keep-set
is a function of them. (ii) Pruning is information-free by (i), so AVR and uniform encoding at $E$ induce the same
distribution; the data-processing inequality bounds the remaining difference by the information the bar's encoding
fails to carry. (iii) Token-layer accounting with the information-bearing depth.
\end{proof}

The premise is measured with its violation bounded by Pinsker, $\mathrm{TV}\le\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}/2}$: at the
boundary the answer distribution depends on the token values by at most $0.033$--$0.044$ in total variation, while
the same patch one window earlier moves it by $0.36$--$0.41$ (Appendix~\ref{app:mech}). The horizon is sharp, and it
is the same $0.57\times$ depth the masking experiments locate. The conclusion is what
Appendix~\ref{app:identity} tests: gain tracks headroom with $r=0.966$ over ten cells, and again with $r=0.911$
over eight further cells whose headroom is near zero, where it correctly predicts that AVR does nothing.
\emph{Where it is only approximate:} the premise holds to KL~$\approx5\times10^{-4}$, not to zero, so the
conclusion is approximate too. Measured slippage is at most $0.5$ points in seven of ten cells and $-1.8$ in one.
The theorem's real content is that the \emph{choice} of keep-set is irrelevant, which is why random keep matches
attention keep on $96.3\%$ of items.

\begin{proposition}[The budget determines the affordable resolution]\label{prop:budget}
\emph{Pruning to a fraction $k$ at layer $P$ of $N$ costs $\mathrm{TL}=E(P{+}1)+kE(N{-}1{-}P)$ token-layers.
Matching a uniform bar of $B$ tokens gives $E^\star = BN/\big((P{+}1)+k(N{-}1{-}P)\big)$.}
With $B=600$, $N=28$, $P=16$, $k=0.1$ this is $E^\star=928$, a $1.55\times$ resolution multiplier. We ran $E=900$,
$97\%$ of the optimum, and realised $96$--$100\%$ of the bar. This is arithmetic; we state it because it is the
design equation, and because it says the multiplier is fixed by the boundary's depth fraction rather than chosen.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Value-inertness implies selection irrelevance]\label{prop:sel}
Suppose that for every subset $D$ of visual positions, replacing the hidden values of $D$ at layer $p$ with
arbitrary donor values leaves the option distribution unchanged. Then for any two keep-sets $D_1,D_2$ of equal
size, the models $U_E\circ P_{D_1}$ and $U_E\circ P_{D_2}$ induce the same distribution: every ranking rule is
equivalent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The premise makes the output invariant to the values of any subset of visual positions at layer $p$. The kept set
is such a subset, so no choice of kept set can change the distribution.
\end{proof}
\emph{Validation.} On the unpruned $900$-token pass at the boundary (L16, Qwen3, $n=191$), replacing the hidden
states of the $810$ pruned positions with another image's gives KL $0.0022$ ($1.0\%$ flips) and replacing the $90$
kept positions gives KL $0.0065$ ($3.1\%$); the same patch at L8 gives $0.341$ ($20.9\%$). Qwen2 replicates
($0.0038$/$3.7\%$ and $0.0027$/$0.5\%$ at L16; $0.2622$/$29.8\%$ at L8), so the premise holds on both models.

\begin{proposition}[A single window cannot serve two distant sites]\label{prop:window}
\emph{A window of side $W$ contains at most one of two evidence sites separated by more than $W$.}
Trivial, and it is not the interesting part. The interesting part is empirical: with \emph{perfect} placement,
cross-instance error stays at $25\%$ and $22\%$ against $3.5\%$ and $6.1\%$ for single-instance, and two
full-resolution crops covering both objects still score $-2.6$ and $-3.9$ against one. The residual is not a
perceptual failure, so no reallocation of perceptual resource reaches it --- which is why the scope law is a
property of the question type and not of the method.
\end{proposition}

\section{Method ablations}\label{app:abl}

\textbf{Window size.} $W$ is a first-order hyperparameter, and the right value is set by the same condition that
sets the sign. At an equal answer budget ($300$ vs $300$ tokens, identical items), \vstar\ wants the tight window:
$W{=}0.25$ scores $72.8$ against $W{=}0.5$'s $62.3$ and the full image's $53.9$. On text and documents the
ordering reverses --- the tight window clips words and drops the referent --- and $W{=}0.5$ recovers most of the
gap: TextVQA $49.8 \to 67.9$ and DocVQA $35.2 \to 50.3$ at the same budget, still below the no-crop arms
($74.7$ and $56.0$). A dynamic rule that sizes the window from the ridge score's top-5\% bounding box degenerates
to a wide window (mean $W$ $0.46$--$0.47$; $64$--$71\%$ of items at the $0.50$ clamp) and tracks $W{=}0.5$ rather
than adapting; reported as a negative for that rule, not for dynamism in general.

\textbf{Per-head statistics.} The deployed read-out averages over heads. Adding, per layer, the per-cell maximum
over heads and the head-distribution std ($91$ features instead of $63$) raises out-of-fold coverage from $0.605$
to $0.630$ on Qwen3 and $0.581$ to $0.609$ on Qwen2. End-task the gain is $+1.0$ [$-4.2,+6.3$] on \vstar\ and
$+2.4$ [$-1.0,+5.8$] on TextVQA: positive in both cells, significant in neither. The coverage gain does not
convert --- another instance of the intermediate-metric gap (Appendix~\ref{app:proxy}) --- so the head-augmented
filter is kept as an option rather than as the deployed method.

The last row generalises the rest, and explains why the two policies cannot be merged into one. DWA's signal
is produced after the transport boundary, and pruning only saves compute at or before it. A pipeline that prunes
early enough to save has destroyed the layers DWA reads, which costs $3.1$ and $12.6$ points of coverage; a
pipeline that waits for DWA has nothing left worth pruning. Holding the ranking fixed and moving only the cut
from L16 to L4 costs $5.2$ points. The incompatibility is structural, not a tuning failure.

\paragraph{The two policies are substitutes, not complements.} The structural argument above is not the only
reason they do not combine. We also tested the composition that \emph{is} mechanically sound: hold the token-layer
budget fixed, and let any placement rule spend its answer pass on a crop encoded at $460$ tokens and pruned $90\%$
at the boundary instead of a plain crop at $300$. Applied to ViCrop-style block-mean, to LASER, to DWA and to
oracle placement, on both checkpoints, every one of the eight contrasts spans zero. The diagnostic says why: the
resolution headroom left \emph{on a crop} averages $+0.8$ points, against $+4.5$ on the full image. A
$0.25\times0.25$ window re-encoded at $300$ tokens has already lifted the object above the encoding cliff, so the
magnification AVR would buy has already been spent. Both policies spend the same resource, effective resolution on
the region that matters; cropping spends it in a targeted way and AVR spends it uniformly. That is why their
\emph{scope} is complementary while their \emph{gains} are not additive.

\paragraph{Why cropping cannot reach cross-instance questions.} Under \emph{perfect} placement, cross-instance
accuracy reaches $75.0$ and $77.6$, an irreducible error of $25\%$ and $22\%$, against $3.5\%$ and $6.1\%$ for
single-instance. Two crops at full resolution covering \emph{both} objects, which raises cross-instance object coverage by
$17.1$ and $15.8$ points, still score $-2.6$ and $-3.9$ against a single crop. Cross-instance error is not predominantly perceptual, so no
reallocation of perceptual resources reaches it. Resolution does reach part of it, which is what the second policy
exploits, but cropping does not.

\section{Pipeline faults found, and how they were caught}\label{app:faults}

Two runs in this work produced plausible numbers that were wrong. We report them because the rules that caught them
are the reason we trust the rest, and because both faults are easy to make and invisible in aggregate accuracy.

\paragraph{A silently unpatched module.} Pruning is injected by monkey-patching the attention forward pass. The
patch covered two module paths; a third checkpoint's attention lived in a third module, so pruning was a no-op and
the arm was byte-identical to its unpruned control while reporting a clean $+12.2$. It was caught by a standing
rule: a contrast of exactly $+0.0$ with a $[+0.0,+0.0]$ interval is a pipeline fault, never a result, because real
effects do not land on zero to machine precision. The fix asserts at load time that the model's actual attention
module is one the patch reached, and prints it every run.

\paragraph{A hard-coded depth on a differently-shaped model.} The arm table hard-coded the prune layer at $16$. On
the $36$-layer checkpoint the correct depth, $0.57\times36=21$, was computed and printed but never used, so the run
pruned at $44\%$ of depth, inside the transport window. It was caught by the compute budget reading $78\%$ of the
bar instead of the expected $98\%$. We retain the voided run as accidental evidence for the boundary itself:
pruning inside the window cost $-7.9$ pooled and $-10.5$ cross-instance on a third model family.

Every arm therefore prints its realised token count and its token-layer budget as a fraction of the bar, and any
run outside $96$--$104\%$ is discarded rather than reported.
