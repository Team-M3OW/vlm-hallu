\begin{abstract}
Vision--language models on high-resolution imagery must decide where to spend a fixed visual-token budget;
evidence not given resolution is destroyed before the language model runs. The prevailing remedy is
attention-guided cropping: read the model's own attention over image positions, crop where it peaks, re-encode.
Every such method must choose a depth at which to read that map, and published methods hand-pick a single layer or
average a fixed block.
We show that this choice dominates every other design decision, and that the depth these methods read is not the
depth at which the image reaches the answer. Masking text-to-image attention layer by layer localises that transport
to the first two thirds of the stack --- blocking every layer from layer~16 of~28 onward flips no answers --- at
the same fraction of depth, $0.57$, on every architecture we test, entirely before the band these methods read.
Two policies follow from that measurement rather than from tuning. \textbf{Transport-Weighted Read-out} (TWR)
replaces layer selection with one closed-form ridge solve over the whole depth profile, yielding a signed filter
that subtracts the late layers a conventional read-out adds. \textbf{Transport-Scheduled Resolution} (TSR) discards
$90\%$ of visual tokens at the boundary, where they are causally inert, and spends the freed compute on encoding
resolution.
At matched compute TWR beats every published placement rule in all nine pooled comparisons, by $7.9$ and $8.9$
points over the strongest, and lands its crop on the evidence $63.9\%$ and $55.0\%$ of the time against $46.6\%$ and $39.3\%$. TSR converts a
checkpoint's resolution headroom at rate $1.01$ ($r=0.966$) across four checkpoints and three benchmarks. Against
models at their \emph{native} dynamic resolution --- $5.5\times$ the budget --- both are statistically
indistinguishable using $14$--$18\%$ of the visual-token compute, whereas published rules fall $9.9$--$14.2$ points
short of it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A vision--language model asked ``what material is the glove?'' about a $2250\times1500$ photograph must first encode
the image into visual tokens. At any finite budget the glove may occupy a fraction of a token, and the evidence is
gone before the language model runs. The model still \emph{localises} it --- its attention concentrates on the right
region --- but it cannot \emph{perceive} it.

The standard remedy is attention-guided cropping: run the model once, read its attention over image positions, crop
where the attention peaks, and re-encode that crop at higher density~\citep{vicrop,zoomeye}. Every method in this
family faces one decision that is rarely examined: \textbf{at which layer should the attention map be read?}
Published methods pick a single mid-network layer or average a fixed block of late layers.

We show three things.

\textbf{(i) The depth choice dominates.} Cropping at the peak of one hand-picked mid layer scores $24.6\%$ and
$33.9\%$ on two checkpoints --- the four-option chance rate, and far below not cropping at all ($62.3\%$, $57.4\%$).
The same pipeline with a better depth read-out scores $77.2\%$ and $71.3\%$.

\textbf{(ii) Everyone reads at the wrong depth, and we can say why.} Masking text tokens' attention to the image,
layer by layer, shows that image-to-text transport is \emph{complete} by layer~16 of~28: blocking every layer from 16
onward flips no answers. Yet every method in this family --- including ours --- reads its map at layers 17--22. The
attention these methods consume is a residue of where the model looked, not the channel carrying the evidence.

\textbf{(iii) Two allocation policies follow from that measurement}, with complementary strengths, and at native
resolution they match a model spending $5.5\times$ the compute. \textbf{Transport-Weighted Read-out} (TWR,
\S\ref{sec:ridge}) stops selecting a depth and instead re-weights all of them, by a single closed-form ridge solve
whose fitted weights are signed: the post-boundary layers a conventional read-out adds, it subtracts.
\textbf{Transport-Scheduled Resolution} (TSR, \S\ref{sec:tsr}) drops $90\%$ of visual tokens at the boundary, where
they no longer carry the image, and spends the saved compute on encoding resolution instead.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Benchmarks.} \vstar~\citep{vstar} ($n=191$; 115 single-instance, 76 cross-instance) and HR-Bench 4K/8K
\citep{hrbench} ($n=800$ each; 400 per stratum). Both are public; we build no datasets. Questions are four-way
multiple choice, scored by the arg-max over option-letter log-probabilities, so results are deterministic.

\textbf{The equal-compute bar.} A two-pass method that localises at $300$ tokens and answers on a crop at $300$ tokens
spends $600$ visual tokens. We therefore compare against \texttt{uniform@600}: the same budget spent on one
higher-resolution pass. Published work in this area does not budget-match; \S\ref{sec:results} shows what that hides.
Where pruning changes cost mid-network we measure \textbf{token-layers} (tokens $\times$ layers traversed); the bar is
$600\times28=16{,}800$.

\textbf{Stratification.} Every result is reported separately for \emph{single-instance} questions (one object's
property) and \emph{cross-instance} questions (a relation between two). \S\ref{sec:scope} shows these behave
oppositely and that pooling them conceals it.

\textbf{Statistics.} Paired item bootstrap, 8000 resamples. Learned components use GroupKFold(5) by item $\times$ 3
seeds. A claim must hold on at least two checkpoints or it is reported as rejected.

\section{Where the image reaches the answer}\label{sec:transport}

We install a hook that masks a chosen set of query positions from attending to the image-token columns, at chosen
layers only, and measure the KL divergence at the output.

\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\caption{Causal masking of text-to-image attention (Qwen3-VL-2B / Qwen2-VL-7B, $n=40$).}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
mask scope (all layers) & KL at output & answers flipped\\
\midrule
the \textbf{answer token}'s row only & $0.025$ & $3\%$\\
\textbf{all text rows} after the image & $1.931$ & $42\%$ / $45\%$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\label{tab:transport}
\end{table}

Blocking the answer-emission token from seeing any image token, at every layer, changes almost nothing
(Table~\ref{tab:transport}). Visual evidence reaches the answer through the \emph{text} tokens, which consolidated it
upstream. Masking one layer at a time for all text rows localises that consolidation: it peaks at L11 (Qwen3) and
L14--15 (Qwen2), prefix masks $L_0..\ell$ saturate by $\ell=12$--$16$, and suffix masks $L_\ell..\text{end}$ fall to
$\approx0$ from $\ell=16$ ($0.014$ and $0.029$; zero answer flips from $\ell=20$). On a 36-layer checkpoint the same
behaviour appears at L21, i.e.\ the boundary transfers as $0.57\times$ depth.

\paragraph{The consequence.} The read-out band used by every method in this family (L17--20 on Qwen3, L19--22 on
Qwen2) lies \emph{entirely after} the transport window. Those layers' image attention is causally inert
(KL~$\approx0.001$). This predicts, and we confirm, three things: depth \emph{re-weighting} helps while depth
\emph{selection} never does (\S\ref{sec:ridge}); tokens can be dropped at the boundary for free (\S\ref{sec:tsr}); and
placement rules that all read the same inert depth separate only modestly.

\paragraph{Selection stops mattering exactly at the boundary.} Before it, the choice of which tokens to keep is worth
$+8.4$ and $+8.9$ points over random. At and after it, ranking is worth nothing: random keep equals attention keep on
both models at every keep ratio. The boundary is not merely where pruning becomes cheap --- it is where selection
ceases to matter.

\section{Policy I: Transport-Weighted Read-out (TWR)}\label{sec:ridge}

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig_pipe_ridge.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Policy I: Transport-Weighted Read-out (TWR).} One localisation pass, every layer's attention kept, a single
closed-form solve that re-weights depths, and a crop. Nothing is selected: all $28$ layers enter the score, and the
fitted weights are signed, so layers past the transport boundary subtract rather than add.}
\label{fig:pipe_ridge}
\end{figure}


Localise at $300$ tokens with the full prompt (the read-out must be taken at the answer-emission position; without the
answer instruction the map loses $36$--$42$ points). Read the final prompt token's attention over image cells at all
$28$ layers. Score each cell by a single closed-form ridge solve,
\[
s(\text{cell})=\textstyle\sum_\ell w_\ell\log A_\ell+\sum_\ell v_\ell\,\mathrm{rank}_\ell+a\log\mathrm{nb}+\text{geometry},
\]
fit on as few as $50$ boxed examples. Crop $W{=}0.25$ at the arg-max, re-encode at $300$ tokens, answer.

The fitted $w_\ell$ \emph{is} the depth filter (Figure~\ref{fig:filter}). It takes both signs: it assigns
\emph{negative} weight to 7 of the 11 layers the conventional block-mean read-out adds at $+1$, and its largest
positive weight falls in the band where attention first becomes question-conditioned. There is no boosting, no
learning rate, no early stopping, no seed averaging --- given the data the solution is unique.

\paragraph{Why re-weighting and not selection.} On the items the conventional read-out gets wrong, the filter ranks
the target cell above the cell the arg-max chose on $57\%$ and $71\%$ of items, against $13\%$ and $12\%$ for the
block mean. No monotone summary of the depth profile reproduces this --- not magnitude, neighbourhood, isolation, or
a windowed sum --- because the correction requires subtraction, and a mean can only add.

\paragraph{Ablations.} A gradient-boosted tree over the same features is statistically indistinguishable from the
ridge solve on all four architectures, so the closed form costs nothing. Capacity is not the limit at this sample size: a
spatial CNN is \emph{significantly worse} on the 7B ($-8.9$ and $-9.4$ at two window sizes), and nine random seeds,
flip augmentation and ensembling all land inside the noise floor. Roughly $50$ boxed examples recover $70\%$ of the
full-data gain. A training-free variant --- the maximum over the four question-conditioned layers --- reaches $68.9$
and $60.7$, above every published rule but $3.7$ and $9.5$ points below the fitted filter.

\section{Policy II: Transport-Scheduled Resolution (TSR)}\label{sec:tsr}

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig_pipe_tsr.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Policy II, transport-scheduled resolution.} Spend the compute the inert late-layer visual tokens
would have used on resolution inside the window where the image is actually read. The prune depth is read off the
causal measurement rather than tuned, and nothing spatial is discarded before the boundary.}
\label{fig:pipe_tsr}
\end{figure}


Because visual tokens are inert past the boundary, discard them there and spend the saving on pixels: encode at $900$
tokens, run full width through L0--L16, keep the top $10\%$ at L16, run L17--27 narrow. Token-layers
$900{\times}17+90{\times}11=16{,}290$ --- $96\%$ of the bar, for $1.5\times$ the resolution. No crop.

Pruning is free: $0.0$--$0.5$ points in 9 of 10 measured cells, at every keep ratio ($10/25/50\%$), with any ranking
including random. Output probabilities change on $99\%$ of items while the arg-max changes on $2\%$ --- the tokens are
inert, not absent.

\paragraph{An identity.} Across $4$ checkpoints $\times$ $3$ benchmarks, TSR's gain equals the checkpoint's
\emph{measurable resolution headroom} $\mathrm{acc}(900)-\mathrm{acc}(600)$ on that stratum: $r=0.966$, slope $1.01$,
mean absolute error $0.7$ points over 10 stratum-cells. The headroom needs two baseline runs and no method at all, so
TSR's benefit is \emph{predictable before deployment} --- and it explains the cells where TSR gains little: those are
the cells where the checkpoint gains little from resolution in the first place, including one where the baseline
itself degrades from $600$ to $900$ tokens.

\paragraph{The boundary is the right depth, not merely a cheap one.} Pruning the same fraction at layer~2 (the FastV
default) costs $4.7$ points more; a causally-scheduled taper that prunes $50\%$ inside the transport window costs
$5.8$ points more than one cut at the boundary and loses to PyramidDrop's fixed schedule; and pruning at $0.14$ of
depth rather than $0.57$ on the 36-layer checkpoint costs $7.9$ points pooled. Conversely, pruning \emph{after} the
boundary saves less for no additional accuracy. One cut, at the measured boundary, dominates every schedule we tried.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Attention-guided cropping.} \citet{liu2026vea} document the gap this family exploits, that a model's
attention can concentrate correctly while its answer is wrong. ViCrop~\citep{vicrop} crops at the peak of a fixed mid-layer attention map;
ZoomEye~\citep{zoomeye} searches a tree of crops with repeated model queries. VisLens~\citep{vislens} reaches the
same place without attention at all, decoding visual tokens through a tuned logit lens, matching them against the
query, and feeding the crop back alongside the original image in one pass. Every attention-based member fixes the
read-out depth by hand, and none of them budget-match against simply spending the same tokens on resolution ---
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows that at equal compute the family is worth $-1.6$ to $+3.1$ points.

\textbf{Layer-adaptive read-outs.} LASER~\citep{laser} selects the attention layer per sample by contrasting the map
with and without the query, and reports that no single ``magic layer'' suffices --- a conclusion we reach
independently and extend causally. ILVAD~\citep{ilvad} accumulates newly-activated tokens across adjacent layers;
TRACE~\citep{trace} identifies a three-stage depth pattern and reshapes it with trained modules; ViRGo~\citep{virgo}
reports our cross-instance finding (``patch-based zooming \ldots\ can split large objects and destroy global spatial
context'') and resolves it with a three-way router keyed on object scale. We treat these as concurrent work reaching compatible conclusions. Our contribution relative to them is
(i) the causal localisation of the transport window rather than a correlational choice of layer, (ii) a signed
\emph{re-weighting} across all depths rather than a selection among them, and (iii) budget-matched, stratified
evaluation including against native resolution.

\textbf{Depth selection in language models.} DoLa~\citep{dola} contrasts a mature and a premature layer; SLED~\citep{sled}
weights all early layers by gradient alignment; confident-layer decoding stops at an entropy valley near the output.
We port each to this setting and all fail (\S\ref{sec:neg}) for one measurable reason: in these vision--language
models final-layer entropy rises on $54$--$88\%$ of items --- far above the $16\%$ reported for language models ---
yet per-layer accuracy still climbs monotonically to the last layer. There is no better layer to select, which is why
re-weighting works where selection does not.

\textbf{Visual-token pruning.} FastV~\citep{chen2024fastv} prunes at layer~2; PyramidDrop~\citep{pyramiddrop} drops tokens in stages with a
fixed ratio; HiRED~\citep{hired} allocates a budget before the language model. These are evaluated as retention-at-reduced-cost. We
derive the pruning depth from the transport measurement rather than tuning it, and show that at that depth the
\emph{choice} of surviving tokens is irrelevant --- random keep matches attention keep --- while one layer earlier it
is worth $+8.4$ and $+8.9$ points.

\section{What does not work}\label{sec:neg}

Ten designs failed to make cropping serve cross-instance questions, and an eleventh failed to merge the two
policies into a single pipeline. We report them because the diagnoses
constrain the design space more than the successes do. Each failed for a measured reason: per-item window
sizing; peak-spanning and mass-containment windows; joint (cell, window) re-ranking under a coverage objective (a
metric artefact --- coverage is monotone in window size); expected-accuracy window selection; a downscaled scene
composed with the crop, which dilutes it by $8.7$ and $5.2$ points \emph{even at oracle placement}; multi-crop at matched
budget, where the second crop genuinely converts ($+7.8$\,\ok) but halving each crop costs $-11.3$; and the same at
full resolution funded by pruning. Depth \emph{selection} rules ported from the LLM literature
--- DoLa, SLED, confident-layer decoding, attention-JSD, rank-stability --- all fail, for one reason: in these models
final-layer entropy rises on $54$--$88\%$ of items while per-layer accuracy still climbs monotonically to the last
layer. There is no better layer to select.

\section{Practical guidance}\label{sec:guide}

The two policies have different cost and risk profiles, and the choice does not require a question classifier. The
TWR needs ${\sim}50$ boxed examples, two forward passes and $1.4$--$2.0\times$ wall-clock; it has the higher
ceiling ($+14.8$ on single-instance) and a $6$--$9$ point cross-instance failure mode. TSR needs no supervision, one
forward pass and ${\sim}1\times$ wall-clock; it is positive on both strata in 6 of 7 cells with no significant loss
anywhere, and its benefit is predictable from two baseline runs. Head to head the two are even pooled (mean
difference $+0.2$ points over seven cells). We recommend TSR as the default and TWR where boxed
supervision exists and single-instance perception dominates. We note that TWR's pooled margin over TSR
is concentrated on the two cells on which it was developed.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:lim}

$n=191$ on \vstar\ gives per-stratum CI half-widths near $10$ points; no method is significantly positive on both
strata in any single cell, which is a power limitation as much as a method one. TWR needs ${\sim}50$
boxed examples and two forward passes ($1.4$--$2.0\times$ wall-clock); TSR needs neither. Our two core checkpoints
share a model family. TWR's pooled margin over TSR is concentrated on the two cells it was developed on.
Pruning at the boundary is free in 9 of 10 cells; the exception (HR-Bench~8K, cross-instance, $-1.8$\,\no) is real.

\section{Conclusion}

Attention-guided cropping reads the model's attention at a depth where that attention no longer carries the image to
the answer. Measuring where it does --- a causal masking experiment, not a heuristic --- yields both a better
read-out (TWR) and a free pruning point (TSR), and the two together match a model running at $5.5\times$ the
visual-token budget.
The remaining gap is placement: perfect placement at $600$ tokens exceeds native dynamic resolution by $11.6$ points.

\section{Experimental protocol}\label{app:proto}

\textbf{Models.} Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct (core pair), Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct
(36 layers), LLaVA-NeXT-7B and LLaVA-OneVision-7B (attention analyses only). \texttt{bfloat16}; eager attention
wherever hooks or a prune bias are required, SDPA otherwise. We note a ${\sim}2$ point kernel-dependent difference on
the 7B between the two implementations and always compare arms computed under the same kernel.

\textbf{Budgets.} Images are resized so that the processor's own \texttt{image\_grid\_thw} yields the target token
count ($\pm 8\%$); realised counts are recorded per arm and a contrast is voided if budgets drift by more than $10\%$.
Where pruning changes cost mid-network we report token-layers.

\textbf{Read-out.} The attention map is the final prompt token's attention over image-token columns, averaged over
heads \emph{before} normalising (the order matters: normalising per head first up-weights sink-heavy heads and shifts
the block-mean read-out by $11$ points on the 7B). The prompt must end at the answer-emission point; a prompt ending
elsewhere costs $36$--$42$ points on the arg-max read-out.

\textbf{Statistics.} Paired item bootstrap with 8000 resamples. Learned components use GroupKFold(5) grouped by item
$\times$ 3 seeds; the headline TWR result is additionally reproduced under a disjoint fold-seed set (difference
within $\pm1$ point). Run-to-run floor on coverage is $1$--$2.5$ points.

\textbf{Pipeline discipline.} An exactly $+0.0$ contrast with a $[+0.0,+0.0]$ interval is treated as a pipeline fault,
never a result; this rule caught a monkey-patch that silently failed to reach one model family. Every pruning run
asserts at runtime that the loaded model's attention module is the one that was patched.

\section{Intermediate metrics systematically over-report}\label{app:proxy}

Five times in this work an intermediate metric improved while end-task accuracy did not: a read-out that closed the
coverage gap but converted $3.1$ points worse; a ridge variant that tied on coverage and differed on the chosen cell
for two-thirds of items; an early-exit localiser free in coverage but $-3.7$ pooled at the end task; a
higher-resolution localiser that gained $+4.2$ coverage and $-3.7$ accuracy; and an objectness probe that retains the
evidence box far better than attention ($87\%$ vs.\ $72\%$) and converts to $-1.6$. We therefore report coverage only
as a diagnostic and gate every claim on end-task accuracy at matched compute.

of coverage, while restricting it to $\le$ L16 costs $9.4$ and $22.0$. Transport finishes at the boundary; the
signal the ranking reads is produced after it.

The last row generalises the rest, and explains why the two policies cannot be merged into one. TWR's signal
is produced after the transport boundary, and pruning only saves compute at or before it. A pipeline that prunes
early enough to save has destroyed the layers TWR reads, which costs $3.1$ and $12.6$ points of coverage; a
pipeline that waits for TWR has nothing left worth pruning. Holding the ranking fixed and moving only the cut
from L16 to L4 costs $5.2$ points. The incompatibility is structural, not a tuning failure.

\paragraph{Why cropping cannot reach cross-instance questions.} Under \emph{perfect} placement, cross-instance
accuracy reaches $75.0$ and $77.6$, an irreducible error of $25\%$ and $22\%$, against $3.5\%$ and $6.1\%$ for
single-instance. Two crops at full resolution covering \emph{both} objects, which raises cross-instance object coverage by
$17.1$ and $15.8$ points, still score $-2.6$ and $-3.9$ against a single crop. Cross-instance error is not predominantly perceptual, so no
reallocation of perceptual resources reaches it. Resolution does reach part of it, which is what the second policy
exploits, but cropping does not.

\section{Pipeline faults found, and how they were caught}\label{app:faults}

Two runs in this work produced plausible numbers that were wrong. We report them because the rules that caught them
are the reason we trust the rest, and because both faults are easy to make and invisible in aggregate accuracy.

\paragraph{A silently unpatched module.} Pruning is injected by monkey-patching the attention forward pass. The
patch covered two module paths; a third checkpoint's attention lived in a third module, so pruning was a no-op and
the arm was byte-identical to its unpruned control while reporting a clean $+12.2$. It was caught by a standing
rule: a contrast of exactly $+0.0$ with a $[+0.0,+0.0]$ interval is a pipeline fault, never a result, because real
effects do not land on zero to machine precision. The fix asserts at load time that the model's actual attention
module is one the patch reached, and prints it every run.

\paragraph{A hard-coded depth on a differently-shaped model.} The arm table hard-coded the prune layer at $16$. On
the $36$-layer checkpoint the correct depth, $0.57\times36=21$, was computed and printed but never used, so the run
pruned at $44\%$ of depth, inside the transport window. It was caught by the compute budget reading $78\%$ of the
bar instead of the expected $98\%$. We retain the voided run as accidental evidence for the boundary itself:
pruning inside the window cost $-7.9$ pooled and $-10.5$ cross-instance on a third model family.

Every arm therefore prints its realised token count and its token-layer budget as a fraction of the bar, and any
run outside $96$--$104\%$ is discarded rather than reported.
