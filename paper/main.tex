\begin{abstract}
Vision-language models (VLMs) can often localize relevant regions in an image, yet still fail to perceive the fine-grained visual evidence needed to answer correctly, leading to visual hallucinations. Using more tokens provides a finer representation of the image, but also increases the computation, memory, and latency required for inference. VLMs therefore operate under a limited visual-token budget: spending more of it on irrelevant details wastes computation without improving the answer. The central challenge is therefore to spend this limited budget where additional visual detail is most useful.

A common strategy is attention-guided cropping, however, existing approaches typically extract this attention from a manually selected layer or a fixed range of layers, without establishing which depth provides the most reliable signal for visual selection. We show that the choice of layer is a dominant factor in attention-guided visual selection. By systematically masking text-to-image attention across network depth, we show that the information relevant to the model's prediction is concentrated within an early-to-middle portion of the network. This boundary occurs substantially earlier than the layers used by existing attention-based selection methods. Based on this finding, we introduce two complementary strategies. Depth-Weighted Attention (DWA) combines attention information across the full depth of the model through a closed-form ridge regression, learning layer weights that suppress less informative late-layer contributions and produce more reliable spatial selection. Adaptive Visual Resolution (AVR) uses the identified depth boundary to remove visual tokens that no longer affect the prediction and redirects the saved computation toward higher-resolution encoding, allowing the model to represent important image regions with finer visual detail.

Across nine matched-compute comparisons, DWA consistently improves over existing layer-selection strategies, achieving gains of 7.9 and 8.9 percentage points over the strongest baseline and locating answer-relevant evidence in 63.9\% of cases compared with 13.6\%. Against native dynamic-resolution models operating at 5.5$\times$ the visual-token budget, DWA and AVR achieve statistically indistinguishable performance while using only 14--18\% of the visual-token computation.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A vision--language model asked ``what material is the glove?'' about a $2250\times1500$ photograph must first encode
the image into visual tokens. At any finite budget the glove may occupy a fraction of a token, and the evidence is
gone before the language model runs. The model still \emph{localises} it, its attention concentrates on the right
region but it cannot \emph{perceive} it.

The standard remedy is attention-guided cropping: run the model once, read its attention over image positions, crop
where the attention peaks, and re-encode that crop at higher density~\citep{vicrop,zoomeye}. Every method in this
family faces one decision that is rarely examined: \textbf{at which layer should the attention map be read?}
Published methods pick a single mid-network layer or average a fixed block of late layers.

We identify the depth at which image information reaches the answer and find that attention-guided cropping methods extract their signal entirely after this point. Masking text tokens' attention to image tokens layer by layer shows that image-to-text transport occurs within the first two thirds of the network. The answer-emission token does not directly attend to the image; instead, it receives information through text tokens that have aggregated visual evidence earlier in the network. Blocking all image access after layer 16 of 28 does not change any answers. The same transition occurs at a normalized depth of 0.57 in both the 28-layer and 36-layer models.


Three findings follow from these measurements:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{(i) The signal used by this family is partly a residual and partly an artifact.} The conventional block-mean read-out places its crop in the last column of the token grid, immediately before a raster row-wrap, in $61.8\%$ and $84.8\%$ of items, compared with a $5.0\%$ chance rate. It covers the relevant evidence in only $13.6\%$ of items, whereas our read-out covers it in $63.9\%$.

    \item \textbf{(ii) The reported gains disappear under matched compute.} At equal visual-token budgets, published placement rules provide gains ranging from $-1.6$ to $+3.1$ points relative to using the same budget for higher resolution. They also remain $9.9$ -- $14.2$ points below the same model evaluated at its native dynamic resolution.

    \item \textbf{(iii) We introduce two allocation policies based on these measurements.} Depth-Weighted Attention (DWA) combines attention information across the full depth of the model through a closed-form ridge regression, learning layer weights that suppress less informative late-layer contributions and produce more reliable spatial selection. Adaptive Visual Resolution (AVR) removes $90\%$ of visual tokens at the measured boundary, where the identity of the retained tokens has little effect, and reallocates the saved compute to higher-resolution encoding. Both reach native dynamic-resolution performance using only $14$--$18\%$ of its visual-token compute. The remaining gap is therefore primarily a placement problem rather than a compute problem: a correctly placed $600$-token crop exceeds native resolution by $11.6$ and $16.2$ points, respectively, setting a ceiling that existing placement rules do not approach.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Attention-guided cropping.} ViCrop~\citep{vicrop} uses the peak of a fixed mid-layer attention map to determine where to crop, while ZoomEye~\citep{zoomeye} searches over a tree of candidate crops through repeated model queries. VisLens~\citep{vislens} takes a different approach and does not rely on attention. Instead, it decodes visual tokens using a tuned logit lens, matches them against the query, and feeds the selected crop back together with the original image in a single pass. A common limitation of these approaches is that the read-out depth is fixed manually, and their evaluations do not control for the compute that could instead be spent on simply increasing image resolution.

\textbf{Layer-adaptive read-outs.} LASER~\citep{laser} selects an attention layer separately for each sample by comparing attention maps obtained with and without the query. Their results suggest that there is no single `magic layer,'' a finding that we reach independently and investigate further from a causal perspective. ILVAD~\citep{ilvad} aggregates newly activated tokens across neighboring layers, while TRACE~\citep{trace} identifies a three-stage pattern across depth and modifies it using trained modules. ViRGo~\citep{virgo} also reports a finding consistent with our cross-instance analysis: `patch-based zooming \ldots can split large objects and destroy global spatial context.'' It addresses this issue with a three-way router based on object scale. We view these works as concurrent efforts that arrive at closely related conclusions. Our approach differs in three main ways: (i) we causally localize the depth range responsible for visual information transport rather than choosing a layer based only on correlation; (ii) instead of selecting a single layer, we learn a signed \emph{re-weighting} across the full network depth; and (iii) we evaluate under matched compute budgets and across stratified settings, including comparisons against native image resolution.

% \textbf{Depth selection in language models.} DoLa~\citep{dola} contrasts representations from a mature layer with those from an earlier layer, while SLED~\citep{sled} weights early layers according to gradient alignment. Confident-layer decoding instead stops generation at an entropy valley near the output. We adapt each of these approaches to vision-language models, but find that none performs reliably in our setting (\S\ref{sec:neg}). The underlying reason is measurable: final-layer entropy increases for 54-88 \% of our examples, substantially higher than the 16 \% reported for language models, while per-layer accuracy continues to improve monotonically through the final layer. In other words, there is no consistently better intermediate layer to select. This helps explain why re-weighting the representations can succeed where layer selection does not.

\textbf{Visual-token pruning.} FastV \citep{chen2024fastv} prunes visual tokens at layer~2, PyramidDrop~\citep{pyramiddrop} progressively removes tokens using a fixed pruning ratio, and HiRED~\citep{hired} determines the token budget before the language model processes the input. These methods are primarily evaluated in terms of how much compute can be saved while retaining performance. In contrast, we derive the pruning depth directly from our measurement of visual information transport rather than treating it as a tunable hyperparameter. At the identified depth, we find that which tokens are retained matters surprisingly little: randomly selected tokens perform comparably to attention-selected tokens. Moving the pruning one layer earlier, however, produces gains of $+8.4$ and $+8.9$ points, highlighting the importance of choosing the pruning depth correctly.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

We first describe how a vision-language model (VLM) processes visual
information and formulate the perception problem
(Sec.~\ref{sec:vlm_vision}), then introduce the framework
(Sec.~\ref{sec:vlm_vision}). We next explain depth boundary
identification (Sec.~\ref{sec:depth_boundary}), followed by
Depth-Weighted Attention (DWA) for spatial selection
(Sec.~\ref{sec:dwa_method}) and Adaptive Visual Resolution (AVR) for
allocating visual computation (Sec.~\ref{sec:avr_method}).

\subsection{What Does a VLM See, and How?}
\label{sec:vlm_vision}

We begin with a frozen VLM comprising a vision encoder \(E\), a
visual-to-language mapping \(P\), and a language decoder \(F_\theta\)
with \(L\) decoder layers. Here, \(P\) represents the model's visual
projection or merging components that produce embeddings for the
language decoder. For a given image \(I\) and question prompt \(q\),
\begin{align}
    V &= P(E(I)) \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}, \\
    \mathbf{z} &= F_\theta(V,q) \in \mathbb{R}^{K}.
\end{align}
Here, \(V\) contains \(N\) visual-token embeddings, each with dimension
\(d\). The decoder \(F_\theta\), including its output head, produces
the vocabulary-logit vector \(\mathbf{z}\) at the final prompt position.
The vocabulary size is \(K\), and \(\theta\) denotes the pretrained model
parameters, which remain frozen. The prompt \(q\) includes the question,
answer options, and answer instruction supplied with the example.

The decoder's logits define the next-token probability distribution
through softmax. We use these logits to evaluate multiple-choice answers,
with the scoring procedure described in Sec.~\ref{sec:method_evaluation}.

\subsection{VLMs and Fine-Grained Perception: Visual Resolution as an Encoding Bottleneck}\label{sec:cliff}

We study whether VLMs can resolve small visual details after correctly localising the relevant region, and whether allocating visual tokens to the relevant region is more effective than uniformly increasing resolution. We first sweep the target extent in merged visual tokens on single-region questions, using an oracle crop as a difficulty control (Appendix~\ref{app:cliff}). We find a sharp, architecture-invariant threshold at 0.15--0.25 merged tokens, corresponding to only 2--3\% of the image side: accuracy jumps from 19.0\% below to 78.6\% above the threshold, while oracle-crop accuracy remains flat (95.8\%/100.0\%). Since question difficulty is unchanged across the split, the cliff reflects a failure to encode the required visual evidence rather than a gradual loss of accuracy with decreasing resolution.

We next localise the source of this failure using layer-wise decoding and representation patching (Appendices~\ref{app:mech} and~\ref{app:encoding}). Localisation alone provides little additional decodability (72.2\% vs.\ 68.1\%), and no layer shows evidence of encoding the answer and subsequently losing it. In contrast, providing sufficient visual detail through an oracle crop corrects 94.4\% of localised-but-wrong cases, despite requiring a median of only 0.14 tokens on the target. Across models, 86.7\%/84.0\% of errors are therefore certified as encoding failures, indicating that the dominant bottleneck is extracting fine-grained visual evidence rather than reasoning over evidence that has already been encoded.

This raises a practical question: should we increase the overall visual-token budget, or allocate the same budget more selectively? We compare oracle-targeted allocation against the best uniform resolution each model can reach, across three architectures at native resolution and the HR-Bench boundary (Appendix~\ref{app:allocation}). Targeted allocation is substantially more efficient: a 300-token oracle crop reaches 97.4\%/93.2\%, while no uniform setting up to 7{,}776 tokens matches it ($\geq$26$\times$/25.9$\times$ more tokens). At native resolution, a 600-token crop likewise outperforms 3,290 uniform tokens by +11.6/+16.2 points. Crucially, the benefit depends on whether the evidence is actually sub-token: targeted allocation improves V*Bench by +37.2 points, where fine details are difficult to resolve, but hurts HR-Bench 4K by $-8.8$ points when the evidence is already resolvable.

We evaluate two complementary strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Depth-Weighted Attention (DWA).}
    DWA uses attention information across decoder layers, together with
    spatial features, to score candidate crop locations through ridge
    regression. The selected region is cropped from the original image
    and re-encoded for answering.

    \item \textbf{Adaptive Visual Resolution (AVR).}
    AVR uses the identified depth boundary to allocate visual-token
    computation toward higher-resolution input. It retains full visual
    access through the early layers and restricts access to selected
    visual tokens afterward.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Identifying the Depth Boundary}
\label{sec:depth_boundary}

We examine where direct access to visual tokens affects answer
prediction. The primary experiment uses Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct~\citep{qwen3vl}
and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct~\citep{wang2024qwen2vl}, each with \(L=28\) decoder layers. It processes
the first \(40\) eligible V*Bench examples in dataset order, skipping
examples without an available, nonempty bounding-box annotation.
Each image is resized toward \(300\) visual tokens. The image, prompt,
and model parameters remain unchanged between its baseline and masked
runs.

We index decoder layers from zero, \(\ell=0,\ldots,L-1\). Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be the set of layers selected for
masking. At these layers, we modify the existing additive attention mask:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{M}^{(\ell)}_{u,v} =
    \begin{cases}
        m_{\min}, & \ell\in\mathcal{S},\;
        u\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{post}},\;v\in\mathcal{V}, \\
        M^{(\ell)}_{u,v}, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Here, \(M^{(\ell)}\) and \(\widetilde{M}^{(\ell)}\) are the original
and modified masks. The indices \(u\) and \(v\) denote query and key
positions. The set \(\mathcal{V}\) is the image-token span, identified
from the input token IDs, and \(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{post}}\) contains
all prompt positions after that span. The value \(m_{\min}\) is the
smallest finite value representable by the mask's data type. This
replacement is applied across attention heads and suppresses the
selected connections after softmax.

We evaluate three masking schedules:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{single}}(b) &= \{b\}, \\
    \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{prefix}}(b) &= \{0,\ldots,b\}, \\
    \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{suffix}}(b) &= \{b,\ldots,L-1\}.
\end{align}
Here, \(b\) is the candidate layer index. Single-layer masking is
evaluated at every layer. Prefix and suffix masks are evaluated at
\(b\in\{0,4,8,12,16,20,24\}\). A separate control masks all layers
together. The suffix mask includes layer \(b\) itself.

We measure the effect on the next-token distribution using
Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence~\citep{kullback1951information}:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{p} &= \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}), \qquad
    \widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathcal{S}} =
    \operatorname{softmax}(\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathcal{S}}), \\
    D_{\mathcal{S}} &= \sum_{k=1}^{K}
    p_k\log\frac{p_k}{\widetilde{p}_{\mathcal{S},k}}.
\end{align}
Here, \(\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathcal{S}}\) is the masked logit
vector. The vectors \(\mathbf{p}\) and
\(\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathcal{S}}\) contain baseline and masked
probabilities over the vocabulary, and \(k\) indexes vocabulary tokens.
The scalar \(D_{\mathcal{S}}\) measures divergence from the baseline
distribution to the masked distribution. We add \(10^{-12}\) inside
the baseline-probability logarithm for numerical stability.

We also record the answer-flip indicator,
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathcal{S}} =
    \mathbf{1}[\hat{c}_{\mathrm{base}}\neq\hat{c}_{\mathcal{S}}],
\end{equation}
where \(\hat{c}_{\mathrm{base}}\) and \(\hat{c}_{\mathcal{S}}\)
are the baseline and masked option predictions under the boundary
experiment's scoring rule in Sec.~\ref{sec:method_evaluation}.
The indicator \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) equals one when its condition holds
and zero otherwise. We average divergence and answer flips across
examples. Answer flips measure changes in the prediction, not changes
in correctness against the ground-truth answer.

The single-layer profile shows where masking has the largest effect.
Prefix masks test the accumulated effect of blocking early layers,
while suffix masks test the need for direct visual access from a
candidate layer onward.
We inspect the suffix divergence and answer-flip
curves to identify where this dependence becomes small. The experiment
does not apply an automatic numerical threshold to select a boundary.
This analysis guides the AVR schedule in Sec.~\ref{sec:avr_method}.
A small suffix effect concerns continued direct access to image tokens;
it does not imply that later layers ignore visual information already
carried by text-token representations. The measured profiles and the
boundary appear in Sec.~\ref{sec:transport_results}; the full tables,
including the 36-layer checkpoint, are in Appendix~\ref{app:depth}.

\subsection{DWA : DEPTH WEIGHTED ATTENTION}
\label{sec:dwa_method}

DWA selects a region using information from the full model layer depth.
We first encode the full image toward \(300\) visual tokens and run
the model with the complete question prompt. At each layer, we extract
the final prompt position's attention to image tokens and average it
across attention heads.

Fig.~\ref{fig:method_dwa} shows the fitted log-attention weights
alongside the single-layer masking response.

    \centering

    \label{fig:method_dwa}

\paragraph{Representing each image cell.}
Let \(a_i^{(\ell)}\) denote the head-mean attention at image cell \(i\) and layer \(\ell\), where
\(i=1,\ldots,N\) and \(\ell=0,\ldots,L-1\), read from the final prompt token over the image cells. We normalise
each map over the image cells:
\begin{equation}
    A_i^{(\ell)} =
    \frac{a_i^{(\ell)}}
    {\max\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_j^{(\ell)},\epsilon\right)},
    \qquad \epsilon=10^{-12},
\end{equation}
where \(j\) indexes image cells in the sum and \(\epsilon\) prevents division by zero. Each cell is represented
by its normalised depth profile,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_i =
    \bigl(\log(A_i^{(\ell)}+\epsilon)\bigr)_{\ell=0}^{L-1}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{L},
\end{equation}
so with \(L=28\) each cell has \(28\) features. The read-out is a single closed-form ridge regression on these
features,
\begin{equation}
    w = (X^\top X + \alpha I)^{-1} X^\top y, \qquad \alpha=1,
\end{equation}
with an unpenalised intercept, to the
coverage label \(y_i\), the fraction of the ground-truth box inside the \(W{=}0.25\) window centred at cell
\(i\). Placement is the ring-masked arg-max of the score
\(s(c) = w_0 + \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1} w_\ell \log A_\ell(c)\).

The feature engineered read-out additionally carries rank, neighbourhood and geometry features; they are specified in
Ablations Section~\ref{app:feat_engine}, and a leave-one-group-in ablation shows the log-attention block alone carries the
advantage.

\paragraph{Learning crop scores.}
The supervision target measures how much of the annotated evidence a
crop would cover:
\begin{equation}
    r_i = \frac{\operatorname{area}(C_i\cap B)}
    {\max(\operatorname{area}(B),\epsilon)}.
\end{equation}
Here, \(C_i\) is the candidate crop centred at cell \(i\), after
shifting it inside the image boundaries. The box \(B\) is the smallest
axis-aligned rectangle enclosing all annotated boxes for that example.
The operator \(\operatorname{area}\) measures area in normalised image
coordinates, and \(r_i\) is the fraction of \(B\) covered by the crop.
This is box coverage, rather than intersection over union.

We standardise each feature using the training-fold mean and standard
deviation, adding \(10^{-9}\) to the standard deviation. Ridge
regression~\citep{hoerl1970ridge} then fits the crop-coverage target:
\begin{equation}
    (\mathbf{w}^{\star},\beta^{\star}) =
    \arg\min_{\mathbf{w},\beta}
    \left\{
    \|X\mathbf{w}+\beta\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{r}\|_2^2
    +\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2
    \right\}, \qquad \lambda=1.
\end{equation}
Here, \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times(2L+7)}\) contains the standardised
features of the \(M\) training cells, and \(\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^M\)
contains their coverage targets. The vector \(\mathbf{w}\) contains
the regression coefficients, \(\beta\) is an unpenalised intercept,
and \(\mathbf{1}\) is a length-\(M\) vector of ones. The notation
\(\|\cdot\|_2^2\) denotes the sum of squared entries, and
\(\arg\min\) returns the fitted parameters, marked by a star.
The coefficient penalty has fixed strength \(\lambda=1\).
We fit the regression coefficients and intercept by solving the
corresponding linear system directly.

The evaluation uses five-fold splits grouped by example, so all cells
from one example stay in the same fold. The procedure is repeated with
seeds \(700\), \(701\), and \(702\), and the three held-out scores
are averaged for each cell. Standardisation and coefficient fitting
use only the training portion of each split.

\paragraph{Selecting and re-encoding the crop.}
We select the eligible cell with the highest averaged score. The outer
grid ring is excluded when both grid dimensions exceed two cells;
otherwise, every cell is eligible. The crop spans \(25\%\) of the
original image width and \(25\%\) of its height. It is shifted inward
when necessary to remain within the image, and its pixel coordinates
are converted to integers. The crop is then re-encoded toward \(300\)
visual tokens and used with the original question prompt for answering.

\subsection{Allocating Visual Computation with AVR}
\label{sec:avr_method}

AVR examines whether later visual-token processing can be reduced while
retaining higher-resolution input in the earlier layers. The main
configuration encodes the full image toward \(900\) visual tokens,
retains unrestricted processing through layer \(16\), and restricts
visual access from layer \(17\) onward. No crop is applied.

\paragraph{Selecting tokens for continued access.}
For a final unrestricted layer \(b\), visual tokens are ranked using
\begin{equation}
    s_i^{\mathrm{AVR}} =
    \frac{1}{b-\max(0,b-4)+1}
    \sum_{\ell=\max(0,b-4)}^{b} A_i^{(\ell)}.
\end{equation}
Here, \(s_i^{\mathrm{AVR}}\) is the selection score for image token
\(i\), and \(A_i^{(\ell)}\) is the normalised, head-averaged attention
from the final prompt position, computed on the higher-resolution
input. With \(b=16\), the score averages layers \(12\)--\(16\).
We retain the highest-scoring tokens, with the count given by
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{keep}} = \max(1,\operatorname{round}(\rho N)),
    \qquad \rho=0.10.
\end{equation}
Here, \(N\) is the realised input visual-token count,
\(N_{\mathrm{keep}}\) is the retained count, \(\rho\) is the retained
fraction, and \(\operatorname{round}\) denotes rounding to the nearest
integer, with ties rounded to the nearest even integer. Additional configurations use
layer-\(16\)-only attention or random selection at the same retained
fraction.

\paragraph{Evaluating the restriction.}
The evaluation first collects attention in an unmasked forward pass.
It then runs the model again with an additive bias of \(-10^4\) on
discarded visual-key columns in layers \(b+1,\ldots,L-1\). The bias
applies to all query positions. It suppresses access to those visual
keys while leaving the token tensors at their original lengths.

The input-token target, retained fraction, and cutoff are fixed for
each evaluated configuration; the main experiment does not use a
per-example rule to choose the input resolution.

\paragraph{Comparisons and transfer.}
We compare DWA with an uncropped image at the reference budget, a
fixed-layer crop at layer~14, and a block-mean attention crop. The latter
two are placement controls within the same crop-only pipeline, rather
than reproductions of the complete ViCrop method~\citep{vicrop}.
The LASER-based control~\citep{laser} uses contrastive attention and
per-example layer selection, but retains our quarter-width crop and
omits LASER's contrastive decoding. It selects the layer with the largest
Euclidean norm of the positive difference between normalised head-mean
query and no-query maps. The no-query map requires an additional
300-token pass, which is excluded from the nominal 600-token placement
accounting. Ground-truth-guided crops serve as
an oracle-placement reference, not a deployable method.

For cross-benchmark evaluations, ridge coefficients are fitted on
V*Bench and transferred within the same checkpoint without target
bounding-box supervision. The dedicated HR-Bench transfer protocol standardises features over
the unlabelled target set; the extended benchmark protocol standardises
them within each image. These transfer runs are reported separately from
the grouped, out-of-fold V*Bench comparison. The extended AVR runs use
random retention of 10\% of visual tokens, with the first restricted
layer at $\operatorname{round}(0.57L)$; this differs from the
attention-ranked core schedule in Sec.~\ref{sec:avr_method}.

\paragraph{Statistical comparisons.}
We use paired item bootstrap intervals~\citep{efron1979bootstrap}
with 8,000 resamples and report 95\% confidence intervals for accuracy
differences. All differences are in percentage points. We identify a
difference as statistically significant when its interval excludes zero. The repeated grouped splits for DWA are described in
Sec.~\ref{sec:dwa_method}.

\paragraph{Answer scoring.}
We evaluate DWA and AVR using next-token logits at the final prompt
position. For each answer option, we combine the scores of the token IDs
obtained from its plain and space-prefixed letter:
\begin{align}
    g_c &= \log\sum_{k\in\mathcal{J}_c}\exp(z_k), \\
    \pi_c &= \frac{\exp(g_c)}
    {\sum_{c'\in\mathcal{C}}\exp(g_{c'})}, \qquad
    \hat{c}=\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\pi_c.
\end{align}
Here, \(\mathcal{C}=\{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B},\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}\}\)
is the set of answer options, indexed by \(c\) and \(c'\).
The set \(\mathcal{J}_c\) contains the distinct final token IDs
returned when tokenising the plain and space-prefixed forms of letter
\(c\) without special tokens. The quantity \(z_k\) is the logit for
vocabulary token \(k\), \(g_c\) is the combined option score,
\(\pi_c\) is its scalar probability after normalisation over the four
options, and \(\hat{c}\) is the predicted option.

The depth-masking experiment instead uses one token ID per option:
the first ID returned when the plain letter is encoded without special
tokens. Its answer prediction is the option with the largest logit
among those four IDs. Its primary divergence measure covers the full
vocabulary; it also records four-option divergence for the all-layer
and single-layer interventions.

For CV-Bench and the multiple-choice portion of RealworldQA, the
option-letter score is evaluated over the supplied choices and also
includes the final token ID of the opening-parenthesis form, such as
\texttt{(A}. Open-answer RealworldQA items use greedy generation of at most
eight tokens and exact matching after lowercasing and removing
punctuation, articles, and extra whitespace.

\paragraph{Performance and data use.}
Answer accuracy measures agreement between the predicted option and
the ground-truth label. Crop localisation is evaluated using the box
coverage defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:dwa_method}. Where a binary coverage
hit is reported, a crop must cover at least half of the enclosing box.
We draw the boundary-analysis examples from V*Bench without a separate
held-out split for boundary selection.

\paragraph{Resolution targets and computation accounting.}
We resize images with bicubic interpolation toward a target visual-token
count and check the count using the model's image processor. We allow up
to six resizing attempts and keep the closest candidate. The relative
stopping tolerance is \(8\%\) for the depth analysis and DWA, and
\(6\%\) for AVR. Target counts therefore need not equal realised counts.

The reference full-image input targets \(600\) visual tokens. DWA's
budget includes both passes described in Sec.~\ref{sec:dwa_method}.
For an AVR token-removal schedule, the visual token-layer proxy is
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{TL}} = N(b+1)
    +N_{\mathrm{keep}}(L-b-1).
\end{equation}
Here, \(C_{\mathrm{TL}}\) counts visual tokens multiplied by the
number of layers through which they would be processed. The first
term represents full-width processing through layer \(b\), and the
second represents processing of retained tokens afterward.
For exactly \(900\) input tokens, \(b=16\), \(L=28\), and
\(N_{\mathrm{keep}}=90\), this gives \(16{,}290\) visual
token-layers, compared with \(16{,}800\) for exactly \(600\) tokens
across all \(28\) layers. We compute this proxy using the realised
token counts.

This proxy quantifies the visual-token processing of the specified
token-removal schedule. Vision-encoder and text-token processing lie
outside its scope. The masking evaluation in Sec.~\ref{sec:avr_method}
measures the schedule's effect on accuracy while retaining full-length
token tensors; $C_{\mathrm{TL}}$ is the cost assigned to physical
token removal.

Three further properties of the late stack follow from these measurements and are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:late}. First, the late layers are \emph{agnostic to the image}: a suffix mask at layer~16 costs $0.014$/$0.029$ KL with $12\%$/$5\%$ answer changes and nothing at all from layer~24, replacing the image tokens' values changes the output no more than irrelevant early text ($0.009$/$0.005$ at L16, $0.0005$/$0.0008$ at L20), and masking only the answer-emission token's image access changes $2.5\%$/$0\%$ of answers. Second, \emph{image and text tokens merge} in the late stack: the patching controls invert across depth (at L4 the image matters $151\times$/$131\times$ more than text; at L20 the text matters $2492\times$/$965\times$ more than the image), and the answer position reads the image only through the text representations that consolidated it. Third, the \emph{layers disagree} about where to look: mean arg-max agreement across layer pairs is $13.8\%$/$38.1\%$, the agreement matrix splits into two clusters at the boundary, and only the layers after it carry a usable placement signal (coverage of the evidence box is at most $20\%$/$5\%$ before the boundary, $41$--$56\%$/$33$--$53\%$ just after it, decaying toward the output). Reading attention at a depth that carries the image is therefore not the same as reading it where placement is decodable, and the two occupy disjoint ranges. \section{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

\subsection{Choice of Read-out Depth}
\label{sec:depth_ablation}

We vary the attention layer while keeping the crop size, answering
procedure, and visual-token budget fixed.
Fig.~\ref{fig:layer_ablation} shows a 35.6-point accuracy range on
Qwen3-VL-2B and a 26.7-point range on Qwen2-VL-7B. The best layers
are 17 and 21, respectively. Every tested read-out before layer~16
performs below the uncropped reference on both checkpoints. This
separates the depth at which image access affects the answer from the
depth at which the final prompt position supplies a useful spatial map.

DWA exceeds the best single layer by 2.6 points on Qwen3-VL-2B and
7.3 points on Qwen2-VL-7B. Only the latter difference has a paired
interval excluding zero. Since the best layer is selected using
benchmark accuracy, this is an optimistic single-layer reference.

\subsection{What Does the Fitted Read-out Contribute?}
\label{sec:weight_ablation}

\paragraph{Weight signs.}
Constraining the 56 log-attention and rank coefficients to be
non-negative produces no statistically significant end-task loss
relative to DWA on either core checkpoint. The spatial coefficients
and intercept remain unconstrained. The corresponding localisation fits set 44
and 43 of the 56 layer coefficients to zero, concentrating weight
on a sparse subset of layers.

\paragraph{A training-free aggregation control.}
Taking the maximum over layers 17--20 on Qwen3-VL-2B and
19--22 on Qwen2-VL-7B gives
68.9\% and 60.7\% accuracy, compared with DWA's 72.6\% and 70.2\% in
the matched comparison. Fitting gives higher point accuracy on both
checkpoints, with a significant advantage on Qwen2-VL-7B.

\paragraph{Fold assignment.}
Refitting with disjoint fold seeds changes pooled accuracy by at most
one point on each checkpoint, with both paired intervals including
zero. DWA is therefore stable across the tested grouped partitions.

\subsection{AVR Cutoff, Retained Tokens, and Input Resolution}
\label{sec:avr_ablation}

\paragraph{Cutoff and token ranking.}
Before the measured transition, attention-ranked retention exceeds
random retention by 8.4 and 8.9 points in the reported comparison.
At later cutoffs, the ranking advantage decreases. In the full
Qwen3-VL-2B V*Bench run, attention and random retention give 70.2\%
and 68.6\% accuracy and agree on 96.3\% of answers. A schedule that begins
reducing visual access within the sensitive depth range loses 1.0
and 5.8 points relative to the boundary-based schedule. These controls
support retaining visual access through the sensitive range and
applying the restriction after the transition.

\paragraph{Higher resolution versus restriction alone.}
We compare AVR with the unmodified model at the same 900-token input
resolution. Across the ten evaluated benchmark--checkpoint--stratum
combinations, seven accuracy differences are within 0.5 points. Two
are positive by 1.3 and 2.6 points, while HR-Bench 8K cross-instance
shows a significant loss of 1.8 points [$-3.2,-0.5$]. Later
restriction thus keeps accuracy close to the unrestricted
higher-resolution model across the evaluated settings.

the same-resolution control, this identifies additional input detail
as the main source of AVR's accuracy gain. The later restriction
allocates the token-layer budget toward that detail.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Decoder depth determines both when direct image access influences
prediction and where attention provides a useful spatial read-out.
Our interventions and layer sweeps separate these roles. DWA uses
fitted depth weights to improve crop placement, while AVR preserves
full-image coverage and concentrates visual-token processing in the
earlier decoder layers.

DWA improves local-detail answering, and its advantage over block-mean
crop selection extends across the evaluated architectures. AVR better preserves performance on tasks
requiring evidence from multiple regions. Together, these findings
provide an empirical basis for choosing spatial concentration or
full-image coverage according to the required evidence. Future work
will optimize physical token removal and evaluate end-to-end latency
and memory, alongside adaptive selection of the spatial allocation policy.
